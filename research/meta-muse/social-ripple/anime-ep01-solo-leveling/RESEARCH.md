# PART A — Solo Leveling (anime) story facts

**Production:** *Solo Leveling* TV anime by A-1 Pictures, directed by Shunsuke Nakashige, adapted from Chugong's Korean web novel (and the webtoon).
- Season 1: 12 episodes, aired **Jan 7 – Mar 31, 2024** (JST; listed as Jan 6 at 24:00). Between Eps 7 and 8 there was a recap special, "How to Get Stronger" (Feb 25, 2024). It is not numbered as an episode.
- Season 2, *Arise from the Shadow*: Eps 13–25, aired **Jan 5 – Mar 30, 2025**.
- Crunchyroll licenses it outside Asia.

**Sources:**
- Wikipedia, "List of Solo Leveling episodes" and "Solo Leveling season 2" (episode titles, dates, summaries; it cites the official site sololeveling-anime.net and Crunchyroll): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Solo_Leveling_episodes · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solo_Leveling_season_2

## A1. Jinwoo at the start (Ep 1 "I'm Used to It"; backstory in Ep 3)
- **CONFIRMED:** Sung Jinwoo is an **E-rank** hunter, the lowest rank. Ranks go E, D, C, B, A, S, and in this world a hunter's rank normally never changes after awakening. His nickname is **"the Weakest Hunter of All Mankind"**. "The world's weakest hunter" is a fine paraphrase.
- **CONFIRMED (why he hunts):** His mother, Park Kyung-hye, is comatose in hospital with the "Eternal Sleep Disease". His father is missing. Jinwoo is the earner who pays her **hospital bills** and supports his younger sister **Sung Jinah**.
  - Ep 1 states his family situation as the reason he casts the deciding vote to enter the second dungeon ("he needs the money").
  - In Ep 3, flashbacks show he became a hunter to earn money for those bills.
  - Jinah debuts in Ep 3. She threatens to quit school and work so he can stop hunting.

## A2. The Double Dungeon and becoming a "Player" (Eps 1–3)
- **Ep 1 (CONFIRMED):** After a routine D-rank raid, a hidden **second dungeon** opens, so it is a "double dungeon". Jinwoo casts the tie-breaking vote to go in. They find a temple full of giant statues with a huge "Statue of God" on a throne. The statues come alive and start killing the hunters.
- **Ep 2 "If I Had One More Chance" (CONFIRMED):**
  - The temple's rules ("commandments") are worded roughly as:
    1. worship/revere God;
    2. praise God;
    3. prove your faith.
  - The wiki adds that a fourth line says those who disobey "shall not be spared".
  - Wikipedia's wording is "revere God / praise God / prove your faith to God". Exact subtitle wording differs between sources, so **paraphrase**.
  - Jinwoo works the rules out. He stays on the altar so the others (Mr. Song and Joohee) can escape.
  - As he is dying, a message tells him he has completed a hidden/secret quest. The wiki names it **"Courage of the Weak"**. He is offered the chance to become a **"Player"**; the wiki adds that his heart would stop in 0.02 seconds if he declined. He accepts.
- **Ep 3 "It's Like a Game" (CONFIRMED):**
  - He wakes in hospital after **three days**.
  - Hunters Association inspectors test him for a "second awakening" (a re-awakening). The test comes back **negative**: the wiki says the mana reading was 10.
  - He then sees the **System** messages, which only he can see.

## A3. The Daily Quest (first in Ep 3)
- **CONFIRMED contents:** 100 push-ups, 100 sit-ups, 100 squats, and a 10 km run. The wiki lists the time limit as 24 hours (it is daily).
- **CONFIRMED warning:** The System warns that failing to comply brings a penalty.
- **CONFIRMED penalty:**
  - Jinwoo dismisses the quest as a hallucination and goes back to sleep.
  - When the timer runs out, he is pulled into the **Penalty Zone**: an endless desert.
  - His penalty quest is to **survive for 4 hours** while **giant (poison-fanged/poison-toothed) desert centipedes** chase him. The wiki spells the name inconsistently.
  - He barely survives.
- **CONFIRMED reward:** The wiki's quest page lists **Status Recovery** (full recovery), **+3 stat points**, and a **random loot box**.
  - After the Penalty Zone, he starts doing the quest every day.
  - In Ep 3 he puts **all his stat points into Strength**.
  - A reward box gives him an **instance-dungeon key** (Hapjeong Station), which leads to his first solo fight.
- **VERIFY, the quest's title:**
  - The fan wiki's quest page is titled "**The Preparation To Become Powerful**". That is the webtoon/novel name, often fan-translated as "Preparations to Become Powerful/Strong".
  - The wiki's Ep 3 summary says the anime's notification reads "**Strength training has arrived**".
  - I could not find a primary source showing the exact English subtitle. Check Ep 3 on Crunchyroll before quoting it on screen. Otherwise paraphrase it as "a daily training quest titled something like 'preparing to become strong'". That is safe because the meaning is the same in every version.
- **Does he keep doing it as he gets stronger? CONFIRMED for Season 1:**
  - **Ep 5:** he jogs his "daily quest run" to a raid meetup.
  - **Ep 7:** the counters keep climbing past the required numbers. He completes **double** the requirement, which triggers a secret reward: a loot box with the **key to the Demon Castle**, an S-rank instance dungeon whose rewards include ingredients for the **Elixir of Life**, a possible cure for his mother. In this episode Jinah jogs with him.
  - **Ep 8:** he does the quest again and tries to "overclock" it. This time it gives nothing special.
  - **Eps 11–12:** he **forgets** the daily quest before the job-change quest. In Ep 12 its timer runs out in the middle of the battle and the System pulls him into the **Penalty Zone**. That accidentally saves him: he recovers, levels up on the centipedes, buys gear (Knight Killer) and potions from the Store, then returns and wins. This is a great beat for the essay: the habit's "penalty" became a lifeline.
  - **Not verified for Season 2:** I did not find a reliable source that the anime shows the daily quest in Eps 13–25. The webtoon chapter pages still list "Daily Quest" as a System feature in the Jeju arc. **Safe line:** "Even once he starts levelling fast, the daily quest stays part of his routine through the first season."

# PART B — Psychology studies

## B1. Lally et al. (2010): how long habits take
**Citation:** Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C. H. M., Potts, H. W. W., & Wardle, J. (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. *European Journal of Social Psychology, 40*(6), 998–1009. https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674 (published online 16 July 2009).

**Sources read:**
- Publisher abstract via Crossref: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/ejsp.674
- Full text from a copy of the publisher's PDF hosted by a third party: https://transformationweightcontrol.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Lally-2010-How-Habits-are-Formed.pdf. Its abstract matches the Crossref abstract word for word. The Wiley page itself was bot-blocked from here.
- Author follow-up: Gardner, Lally & Wardle (2012), *Br J Gen Pract* 62(605):664–666, https://doi.org/10.3399/bjgp12X659466 (PMC3505409).

**Design:**
- 96 volunteers, mostly UCL postgraduate students (30 men, 66 women; mean age 27).
- Each chose one new eating, drinking or activity behaviour and did it once a day in response to a daily cue (e.g. "after breakfast") for 84 days (12 weeks).
- Each day they reported whether they had done it and filled in the Self-Report Habit Index, using its automaticity items.
- An asymptotic curve was fitted to each person's automaticity scores. "Habit formation time" means the modelled time to reach 95% of that person's plateau.

**Exact numbers:**
- 96 recruited. **82** gave enough data; 14 dropped out before day 60.
- The curve could be fitted for **62** people; **39** showed a good fit.
- Median time to 95% of asymptote: **66 days** (IQR **39–102**), range **18–254 days** (n = 39).
- By behaviour type, median days: **eating 65** (n = 10), **drinking 59** (n = 15), **exercise 91** (n = 13). The difference was **not statistically significant** (Kruskal–Wallis p = .328).
- Example behaviours: "eating a piece of fruit with lunch", "drinking a bottle of water with lunch", "running for 15 minutes before dinner".
- **Missed days:** 140 single missed opportunities across 55 people. Automaticity fell by 0.29 points the next day, a very small drop. Over two days, the gain was +0.55 with a miss in between versus +0.79 with no miss.

**Verbatim (abstract):** "The time it took participants to reach 95% of their asymptote of automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days; indicating considerable variation in how long it takes people to reach their limit of automaticity and highlighting that it can take a very long time. Missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process."

**Verbatim (results, p. 1004):** "The median time to reach 95% of asymptote was 66 days, with a range from 18 to 254 days."

**Verbatim (discussion):** "It is notable that the exercise group took one and a half times longer to reach their asymptote than the other two groups. Given that exercising can be considered more complex than eating or drinking, this supports the proposal that complexity of the behaviour impacts the development of automaticity."

**CAUTION, common misreports:**
1. "It takes 66 days to form a habit." No. 66 is the median modelled plateau time for the 39 good-fit participants, with huge spread.
2. The figure is not in the abstract.
3. "18–254 days" is right, but 254 is extrapolated far beyond the 84-day study window.
4. "Complex habits take longer" is the authors' interpretation of a non-significant subgroup difference (91 vs 59–65 days). Say "tended to take longer".
5. Only about 41% of recruits (39 of 96) had a well-fitting curve.
6. "Missing a day doesn't matter" is fine for a single miss. The authors contrast it with an earlier study where missing a week did hurt.
7. The study measured self-reported automaticity, not "habit" as a clinical construct.
8. Jinwoo's quest is far more demanding than the study's behaviours (a glass of water, a piece of fruit, a 15-minute run).

**Safe wording:** "In a UCL study, people who picked one simple daily habit (like drinking water with lunch) took a median of about 66 days for it to feel automatic. But the range was huge, from 18 days to an estimated 254, exercise habits tended to take longer, and missing a single day didn't derail anyone."

## B2. The "21 days" myth: Maxwell Maltz, *Psycho-Cybernetics* (1960)
**Citation:** Maltz, M. (1960). *Psycho-Cybernetics: A New Way to Get More Living Out of Life.* Englewood Cliffs, NJ: Prentice-Hall. I read the Pocket Books reprint of the 1960 text, Preface pp. xiii–xiv and Chapter 7, via its full text on the Internet Archive: https://archive.org/details/maxwell-maltz-psycho-cybernetics-a-new-way-to-get-more-living-out-of-life_202107 (copyright page: "Copyright © 1960 by Prentice-Hall, Inc.").

**What he wrote (verbatim, Preface, under "RESERVE JUDGMENT FOR 21 DAYS"):** "It usually requires a minimum of about 21 days to effect any perceptible change in a mental image. Following plastic surgery it takes about 21 days for the average patient to get used to his new face. When an arm or leg is amputated the 'phantom limb' persists for about 21 days. People must live in a new house for about three weeks before it begins to 'seem like home.' These, and many other commonly observed phenomena tend to show that it requires a minimum of about 21 days for an old mental image to dissolve and a new one to jell."

**Nuance (Ch. 7, "You Can Acquire the Habit of Happiness", practice exercise, verbatim):** "...consciously decide that for the next 21 days you are going to form a new habit by putting on the other shoe first and tying your laces in a different way."

**Independent confirmation of the myth's origin:** Gardner, Lally & Wardle (2012, *BJGP*, PMC3505409): "Some patients may have heard that habits take 21 days to form." / "This myth appears to have originated from anecdotal evidence of patients" / "...typically adjusted psychologically to their new appearance within 21 days." These are three separate fragments as extracted; the words between them were not captured, and the paper's body text is not open access. The same paper advises telling patients to expect habit formation "to take around 10 weeks".

**CAUTION:**
1. Maltz's main claim is about a "mental image" or self-image, and it is a **minimum** ("a minimum of about"), not "21 days to form a habit".
2. His evidence was anecdotal (patients, phantom limbs, a new house), not a study.
3. The flip-side myth, "Maltz never mentioned habits", is also not quite right: he used 21 days for a small "form a new habit" exercise.

**Safe wording:** "The famous '21 days' comes from a 1960 self-help book by plastic surgeon Maxwell Maltz, who wrote that it takes 'a minimum of about 21 days' for a self-image to change, based on what he observed in patients, not on an experiment. Over the years, 'a minimum of about 21 days' got shortened to '21 days to form a habit.'"

## B3. Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006): implementation intentions ("if-then plans")
**Citation:** Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38*, 69–119. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1

**Source read:** the author copy on the University of Konstanz repository (KOPS), a scanned PDF of the published chapter: https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f

**Design:**
- A meta-analysis of studies, from January 1990 to December 2003, in which participants formed an implementation intention: an "if situation Y, then I will do X" plan.
- Each study had to report a statistical link between forming the plan and goal attainment.
- In most studies the comparison group formed only the goal intention ("I intend to...").
- The effect size is Cohen's d (Hedges & Olkin correction).

**Exact numbers:**
- **k = 94 independent tests** from **63 reports**, involving **8,461 participants**.
- Overall **d = .65**, 95% CI **.60 to .70**, which the authors call "medium-to-large".
- By goal domain: laboratory tasks k = 38, health k = 23.
- Large effects for antiracist, prosocial and environmental goals (d = .87, 1.01, 1.12); "medium-sized" effects for health, consumer and personal goals.

**Verbatim (abstract):** "Findings from 94 independent tests showed that implementation intentions had a positive effect of medium-to-large magnitude (d = .65) on goal attainment."

**Verbatim (results, p. 92):** "The overall impact of forming implementation intentions on goal achievement was d = .65 based on k = 94 tests that involved 8461 participants."

**CAUTION:**
1. "If-then plans double or triple your success" comes from single studies. The meta-analysis does not support it.
2. d = .65 is an effect on goal attainment, mostly short-term and often in lab tasks. It is not a measure of long-term habit formation.
3. The effect for health goals was smaller ("medium-sized") than the overall average.
4. Later meta-analyses exist; I did not check them here.

**Safe wording:** "A 2006 meta-analysis of 94 tests, with more than 8,000 people, found that turning a goal into a specific 'if X happens, then I'll do Y' plan had a medium-to-large effect on actually reaching the goal. Jinwoo's quest is basically an if-then plan written for him: every day, these four exercises."

## B4. Amabile & Kramer: the progress principle
**Citations:**
- Amabile, T. M., & Kramer, S. J. (2011, May). The power of small wins. *Harvard Business Review* (reprint R1105C). https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins
- Amabile, T. M., & Kramer, S. J. (2011). *The Progress Principle: Using Small Wins to Ignite Joy, Engagement, and Creativity at Work.* Boston: Harvard Business Review Press.
- **Peer-reviewed paper from the same diary program** (on a different question): Amabile, T. M., Barsade, S. G., Mueller, J. S., & Staw, B. M. (2005). Affect and creativity at work. *Administrative Science Quarterly, 50*(3), 367–403. https://doi.org/10.2189/asqu.2005.50.3.367. Its abstract reports daily diaries from **222 employees in seven companies**.

**Sources read:**
- The full HBR article text, as reprinted in HBR Press's *HBR's 10 Must Reads on Teams*, marked "Originally published in May 2011. Reprint R1105C": https://edelweiss-assets.abovethetreeline.com/BXBC/supplemental/must-reads-teams.pdf
- HBS Working Knowledge summary (Sept 6, 2011): https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-small-wins-unleash-creativity
- Crossref abstract for the *ASQ* 2005 paper.

**Design:**
- Knowledge workers on creative project teams filled in an end-of-day electronic diary survey for the length of their projects.
- The survey asked about emotions, motivation, perceptions of the work environment, and the event that stood out that day.
- The researchers compared the events reported on each person's best days and worst days (judged by mood, emotions and motivation).
- A separate survey asked managers to rank five motivators.

**Exact numbers (HBR):**
- "Twenty-six project teams from seven companies participated, comprising 238 individuals. This yielded nearly 12,000 diary entries."
- "Steps forward occurred on 76% of people's best-mood days. By contrast, setbacks occurred on only 13% of those days."
- Manager survey: 669 managers; "only 35 managers ranked progress as the number one motivator—a mere 5%."

**Verbatim key sentence (HBR):** "...we discovered the progress principle: Of all the things that can boost emotions, motivation, and perceptions during a workday, the single most important is making progress in meaningful work."

Also from HBR: "Even small wins can boost inner work life tremendously. On the flip side, small losses or setbacks can have an extremely negative effect."

**Peer-reviewed vs not:**
- The **progress-principle finding itself** (76% of best days, the manager survey, "single most important") is reported in **HBR and the book**, which are **not peer-reviewed**.
- The underlying diary dataset produced peer-reviewed papers such as *ASQ* 2005, but those papers address other questions, such as affect and creativity.
- Sample-size reporting differs between outlets: 238 in HBR and the book, 222 in the *ASQ* paper.

**CAUTION:**
1. The study is correlational, about workplace "inner work life", not habit formation.
2. "Best days" are the days with the best moods, not the most productive days.
3. HBS Working Knowledge says setbacks had an effect "two to three times stronger" than progress. That is a secondary summary; I did not confirm it in the HBR text.

**Safe wording:** "Harvard researcher Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer analysed nearly 12,000 daily diary entries from 238 professionals. On people's best days, the most common event was simply making progress on meaningful work, even a small step forward. Every check mark on Jinwoo's quest screen is exactly that kind of visible small win."

## B5. Wood, Quinn & Kashy (2002): how much of daily life is habit
**Citation:** Wood, W., Quinn, J. M., & Kashy, D. A. (2002). Habits in everyday life: Thought, emotion, and action. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 83*(6), 1281–1297. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.83.6.1281

**Source read:** the author PDF on Wendy Wood's USC page: https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/Wood.Quinn_.Kashy_.2002_Habits_in_everyday_life.pdf

**Design:**
- Two diary studies with Texas A&M undergraduates:
  - Study 1: **70** students recorded for **1 day**.
  - Study 2: **209** students recorded for **2 days**.
- A wristwatch chimed every waking hour, and each time participants wrote down what they were doing, thinking and feeling.
- A behaviour was classed as a habit if it was performed **"just about every day and usually in the same location"**.

**Exact numbers:**
- Proportion of listed behaviours that were habits: **35% in Study 1, 43% in Study 2**.
- The authors explain the difference: in Study 2, people could list several simultaneous behaviours.

**Verbatim (results, p. 1285):** "between a third and a half of all behaviors listed were classified as habits, given that they were performed just about every day and usually in the same location. This estimate was greater in Study 2 (43%) than in Study 1 (35%)..."

**Verbatim (abstract), the paper's main point:** "When participants were engaged in habitual behavior, defined as behavior that had been performed almost daily in stable contexts, they were likely to think about issues unrelated to their behavior, presumably because they did not have to consciously guide their actions."

**CAUTION:**
1. "43% of everything we do is automatic or unconscious" overstates it. The measure was frequency plus a stable location, among diary-listed behaviours of college students.
2. 43% is only one of the two studies.
3. Wood's later papers cite "about 45%".
4. The 43% figure is not in the abstract.

**Safe wording:** "In one diary study, between a third and a half of the things students wrote down doing were things they did almost every day in the same place. In the larger of two studies it was 43%. And while doing those habits, people's minds were usually somewhere else."
