## A1. Naruto's childhood: why the village shunned him (Eps 1–2)

**CONFIRMED (the cause, Ep 1 prologue):**
- Twelve years earlier, the Nine-Tails attacked the Hidden Leaf village.
- The Fourth Hokage sealed it inside a newborn boy, Naruto, and died doing it.
- Many villagers lost family in the attack. Most of them resented Naruto as if he were the beast itself.

**CONFIRMED (the adults' attitude, Ep 2):**
- On a rooftop, the Third Hokage tells Iruka that people don't change easily.
- He explains that people fear the fox could take over Naruto, or that Naruto **is** the fox.

**CONFIRMED (the rule, Eps 1–2):**
- In Ep 1, Mizuki reveals the secret and says that everyone except Naruto was told never to speak of it. Wikipedia: villagers were "ordered not to tell Naruto".
- Narutopedia's background section, citing manga ch. 2, adds two things:
  - The Third Hokage forbade mention of the Nine-Tails so that the younger generation would not blindly hate Naruto like their parents did.
  - The kids picked up their parents' hostility anyway, **without knowing why**.
- **Story gold:** Naruto was lonely and **did not know the reason** until the night of Ep 1, when he was 12.

**CONFIRMED (no family):**
- He is an orphan who lives alone. Narutopedia: he survived on a monthly allowance from the village.
- Kakashi spells this out to Inari in Ep 12: Naruto grew up without parents and understands Inari's pain better than anyone.

**CONFIRMED (pranks to get attention, Ep 1):**
- Ep 1 opens with Naruto painting graffiti all over the Hokage Rock (the carved faces of the Hokage), with ninja chasing him.
- Narutopedia's Ep 1 synopsis: he "enjoys the attention garnered from reprimands", because he has no parents or friends.
- The character page says his pranks were "for people to notice him, even if it meant by scolding him".
- His dream of becoming Hokage is the same need on a bigger scale: everyone in the village would have to acknowledge him.

**The swing (Ep 1): CONFIRMED that it happens, VERIFY the staging.**
- After failing the clone test (he has failed the graduation exam before; one fan guide says twice, so don't give a number), Naruto sits alone on the swing hanging from the tree outside the Academy.
- Meanwhile the graduates are congratulated by their families.
- Narutopedia lists two mothers in Ep 1, **Tsubaki** and **Ibara**. Both are described as glad that Naruto (the Nine-Tails host) did not graduate.
- The Ep 1 credits also list "Tobio's father" and "Tobio's mother".
- The Academy page confirms the tree with a swing in front of the building.
- **VERIFY by watching Ep 1:** the exact framing (parents patting heads or hugging) and the mothers' whispered lines. Fan reviews describe the scene, but no reliable source transcribes it.
- **Flashbacks:** the swing image comes back in later flashbacks, including in *Shippūden*. I did not pin down specific episode numbers, so **don't cite a flashback episode** unless you have checked it.

**CAUTION:** Narutopedia's Ep 1 synopsis says villagers "abuse him daily". The episode actually shows glares, cold whispers and avoidance. Use "shunned", "ignored" or "looked at coldly". Don't say "beaten".

## A2. Iruka acknowledges Naruto (Ep 1)

**CONFIRMED, in plain words:**
1. Iruka fails Naruto on the clone test. (Earlier in the episode he had scolded him for the graffiti, then treated him to ramen.)
2. Another teacher, Mizuki, tricks Naruto. He says Naruto can still pass if he steals the forbidden Scroll of Seals and learns a technique from it.
3. Iruka finds Naruto in the woods. He realises Naruto was set up.
4. Mizuki attacks and tells Naruto the secret: the Nine-Tails is sealed inside him, which is why the village hates him. Mizuki adds that Iruka must hate him most, because the fox killed Iruka's parents.
5. Naruto runs. Iruka **shields him with his own body** from Mizuki's giant shuriken.
6. Iruka says he never blamed Naruto. He grew up an orphan himself and acted up for attention, so he understands the loneliness.
7. He sees Naruto as one of the village's ninja, not as the fox.
8. Naruto then beats Mizuki with a swarm of shadow clones.
9. Iruka has Naruto close his eyes and ties **his own forehead protector** on him: he graduates.

**Why it matters for the essay:** this is the first time an adult sees *him*, not the monster. Much later, in the war arc, Naruto himself says Iruka was the first person to acknowledge him (Narutopedia, Iruka page).

**Gist line, safe to narrate:** *"He's not the fox. He's Naruto, one of us."* This is a paraphrase. Don't put it in quotation marks as a dub line.

# PART B — Psychology

## B1. Baumeister & Leary (1995): the need to belong
- **Citation:** Baumeister, R. F., & Leary, M. R. (1995). The need to belong: Desire for interpersonal attachments as a fundamental human motivation. *Psychological Bulletin, 117*(3), 497–529. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.117.3.497 (PMID 7777651)
- **Design:** A **theoretical review**, not an experiment.
  - It tests the "belongingness hypothesis" against the existing research.
  - It asks whether people form bonds easily, resist breaking them, suffer when they lack them, and whether one bond can substitute for another.
  - Its key claim: the need has **two parts**. People need frequent pleasant contact **and** an ongoing bond in which they feel cared about.
- **Key numbers:** none. It is a narrative review.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "The need is for frequent, nonaversive interactions within an ongoing relational bond."
- Also from the abstract: "Existing evidence supports the hypothesis that the need to belong is a powerful, fundamental, and extremely pervasive motivation."
- **Source:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7777651/
- **Safe wording:** "Psychologists Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary argued that humans have a basic need to belong. That doesn't mean being around people. It means regular, warm contact inside a bond where someone actually cares about you. A crowded classroom gives you the first part, not the second."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Belonging is as essential as food and water." The paper says "fundamental", not "equal to food".
  - Crediting it to Maslow.
  - Calling it a study with participants.

## B2. Eisenberger, Lieberman & Williams (2003): does rejection hurt?
- **Citation:** Eisenberger, N. I., Lieberman, M. D., & Williams, K. D. (2003). Does rejection hurt? An fMRI study of social exclusion. *Science, 302*(5643), 290–292. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1089134 (PMID 14551436)
- **Design:**
  - **13** UCLA undergraduates (the sample size is in the online methods; ScienceDaily's 2003 release says "13 UCLA undergraduates") played "CyberBall", a virtual ball-toss game, inside an fMRI scanner.
  - They believed they were playing with two other people. In fact it was a preset computer program.
  - There were three scans, always in the same order:
    1. watching only, framed as a "technical problem" (implicit exclusion);
    2. included in the game;
    3. explicit exclusion: after 7 throws they never got the ball again, for about 45 throws.
- **Key numbers (from the paper):**
  - Dorsal ACC was more active in exclusion than inclusion (t = 3.36, r = 0.71, P < 0.005).
  - Self-reported distress correlated with dACC activity: **r = 0.88** (and 0.75 at a second peak).
  - Right ventral prefrontal cortex (RVPFC) was more active during exclusion. Its activity correlated **negatively** with distress (r = −0.68) and with ACC activity (r = −0.81).
  - ACC activity mediated the RVPFC–distress link (Sobel Z = 3.16).
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) was more active during exclusion than during inclusion and correlated positively with self-reported distress."
- **Source:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14551436/ · author-hosted PDF: https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-sanlab/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2016/03/Eisenberger-Lieberman-Williams-2003-Science.pdf
- **Replication and specificity debate (be honest about it):**
  - **Cacioppo et al. (2013)**, *Scientific Reports* 3:2027, https://doi.org/10.1038/srep02027
    - A meta-analysis of Cyberball fMRI studies with **244 participants**.
    - It "fails to support the claim that social rejection operates on the same pain matrix as nociceptive stimuli".
    - It notes that the early studies "were characterized by small sample sizes".
  - **Rotge et al. (2015)**, *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience* 10(1):19–27, https://doi.org/10.1093/scan/nsu110
    - A meta-analysis of **46 articles / 940 subjects**.
    - It did find ACC activation in social pain, in **both ventral and dorsal** ACC.
    - Self-reported distress was tied more to the **subgenual/pregenual** ACC.
    - So "the ACC is involved" holds up. The exact sub-region is debated.
  - **Woo et al. (2014)**, *Nature Communications* 5:5380, https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms6380
    - With **60** participants, fMRI pattern classifiers told pain and rejection apart (92% and 80% accuracy). Each classifier was at chance on the other.
    - The authors conclude that separate representations underlie pain and rejection, despite overlap at the gross anatomical level.
  - **Lieberman & Eisenberger (2015)**, *PNAS* 112(49):15250–5, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1515083112
    - They argue, using Neurosynth reverse inference, that the dACC is selective for pain.
    - **Wager et al. (2016)**, "Pain in the ACC?", *PNAS* 113(18):E2474–5, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1600282113, a ten-author reply, disputed that.
- **Safe wording:** "In a famous 2003 brain-scan study, being left out of a simple online ball game boosted activity in a brain region also involved in the distress of physical pain. The more left-out people felt, the more active it was. Later, bigger studies argue about how close that overlap really is. So 'rejection hurts' is more than a metaphor, but it isn't literally the same as a broken arm."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Rejection is processed exactly like physical pain."
  - "Your brain can't tell the difference."
  - Leaving out that n = 13.
  - Calling the region "the pain center".

## B3. Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton (2010): relationships and mortality
- **Citation:** Holt-Lunstad, J., Smith, T. B., & Layton, J. B. (2010). Social relationships and mortality risk: A meta-analytic review. *PLoS Medicine, 7*(7), e1000316. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316 (PMID 20668659; PMC2910600; open access)
- **Design:**
  - A meta-analysis of **148** prospective studies (**308,849** participants), followed for an average of **7.5 years** (range 3 months to 58 years).
  - It compared survival in people with stronger vs weaker social relationships.
  - "Social relationships" covered many measures: marital status, network size, integration, perceived support, loneliness and so on.
  - All the studies are **observational**. The authors note that causality "is not easily established".
- **Key numbers:**
  - Overall **OR = 1.50** (95% CI 1.42–1.59).
  - Heterogeneity was high: I² = 81%.
  - Complex measures of social integration: OR **1.91** (1.63–2.23).
  - Living alone: OR **1.19** (0.99–1.44), which is **not significant**.
  - Loneliness (inversed): k = 8, OR **1.45** (1.08–1.94).
  - Perceived support: k = 73, OR 1.35.
- **What the 50% means, precisely:**
  - The **odds** of surviving the follow-up period were 1.5 times higher for people with stronger social relationships.
  - This is an **odds ratio**, not "50% less likely to die" and not "lonely people are 50% more likely to die".
  - The PLoS Editors' Summary puts it this way: in a hypothetical 100 people, by the time half have died, there would be **about five more people alive** with stronger social relationships than with weaker ones.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "the random effects weighted average effect size was OR = 1.50 (95% CI 1.42 to 1.59), indicating a 50% increased likelihood of survival for participants with stronger social relationships."
- **Source:** https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2910600/
- **Follow-up paper, loneliness specifically (optional):** Holt-Lunstad, J., Smith, T. B., Baker, M., Harris, T., & Stephenson, D. (2015). Loneliness and social isolation as risk factors for mortality: A meta-analytic review. *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 10*(2), 227–237. https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691614568352
  - Controlled estimates: loneliness OR 1.26, social isolation 1.29, living alone 1.32.
  - In the authors' words, that is "26%, 29%, and 32% increased likelihood of mortality".
  - It found no difference between objective and subjective (felt) isolation.
- **Safe wording:** "A review of 148 studies and over 300,000 people found that those with stronger social ties had about 50% higher odds of still being alive at follow-up. That's a correlation, not proof of cause, but the size of the link is in the same league as well-known health risks."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Loneliness is as deadly as smoking 15 cigarettes a day." **Not in this paper.** Don't attribute it to Holt-Lunstad 2010.
  - "Lonely people are 50% more likely to die."
  - "This study measured loneliness." It measured mostly social *relationships*. Loneliness was 8 of the 148 studies.
  - "Proves friends make you live longer." It is observational.

## B4. Loneliness is not the same as being alone
**Primary definition, Cacioppo lab:** Hawkley, L. C., & Cacioppo, J. T. (2010). Loneliness matters: A theoretical and empirical review of consequences and mechanisms. *Annals of Behavioral Medicine, 40*(2), 218–227. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-010-9210-8 (PMID 20652462; PMC3874845)
- **Design:** A theoretical and empirical review of loneliness's health effects, its mechanisms, and interventions.
  - It sets out the "loneliness regulatory loop": feeling isolated leads to hypervigilance for social threat, then to negative expectations and memories, then to behaviour that confirms the fear.
- **Key numbers (body text):**
  - As many as **80%** of people under 18 and **40%** of adults over 65 report being lonely at least sometimes.
  - For **15–30%** of the general population, loneliness is chronic.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "Loneliness is not simply being alone."
- **Verbatim (body):** "Loneliness is synonymous with perceived social isolation, not with objective social isolation." The text goes on: people can live a socially rich life and feel lonely, or live a fairly solitary life and not feel lonely.
- **Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3874845/

**The "gap" definition, Perlman & Peplau:**
- The classic chapter is Perlman, D., & Peplau, L. A. (1981). Toward a social psychology of loneliness. In S. Duck & R. Gilmour (Eds.), *Personal Relationships 3: Personal Relationships in Disorder* (pp. 31–56). Academic Press. I could **not** get the 1981 text to check the wording word for word.
- I did check the authors' own later encyclopedia entry: Perlman, D., & Peplau, L. A. (1998). Loneliness. In H. S. Friedman (Ed.), *Encyclopedia of Mental Health* (Vol. 2, pp. 571–581). Academic Press. Author PDF: https://peplau.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/141/2017/07/Perlman-Peplau-98.pdf
  - Verbatim: "LONELINESS is the unpleasant experience that occurs when a person's network of social relationships is significantly deficient in quantity or quality."
  - Verbatim (discrepancy model): loneliness occurs "when there is a significant mismatch or discrepancy between a person's actual social relations and his or her needed or desired social relations".
  - Also: "it is the gap between these parameters that is identified as the immediate antecedent of loneliness".
- **CAUTION:** if you cite "Perlman & Peplau (1981)" on screen, use the **paraphrase** (the gap between the connection you want and the connection you have). Don't present the 1998 sentence as a 1981 quote.

**Safe wording:** "Loneliness isn't about how many people are around you. Psychologists define it as the gap between the connection you want and the connection you feel you have. That's why you can feel lonely in a crowd, or in a classroom where everyone knows your name but nobody knows *you*."

**Naruto tie-in:** before Ep 1, Naruto was surrounded by a whole village. He got plenty of attention, even scolding. What he lacked was the second half of Baumeister and Leary's definition: someone who cared about *him*.

## B5. What actually helps: Masi, Chen, Hawkley & Cacioppo (2011)
- **Citation:** Masi, C. M., Chen, H.-Y., Hawkley, L. C., & Cacioppo, J. T. (2011). A meta-analysis of interventions to reduce loneliness. *Personality and Social Psychology Review, 15*(3), 219–266. https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868310377394 (PMID 20716644; PMC3865701)
- **Design:**
  - **50** intervention studies published 1970 to Sept 2009: **12** single-group pre-post, **18** non-randomized comparisons, **20** randomized comparisons.
  - They were analysed separately by design.
  - Four intervention types were compared:
    - (a) improving social skills;
    - (b) enhancing social support;
    - (c) more opportunities for social contact;
    - (d) addressing **maladaptive social cognition**: noticing and testing automatic negative thoughts about others, often with CBT.
- **Key numbers (Hedges' d; negative = less loneliness):**
  - Pre-post studies: **−0.367** (95% CI −0.55, −0.18).
  - Non-randomized studies: **−0.459** (−0.72, −0.20).
  - Randomized studies: **−0.198** (−0.32, −0.08), a small but significant effect.
  - Within the randomized studies:
    - social cognition: **−0.598** (4 studies, p = 0.001);
    - social support: −0.162 (12 studies, p = 0.003);
    - social skills: 0.017 (2 studies, p = 0.90);
    - social access: −0.062 (2 studies, p = 0.67).
  - The difference between types: Q_b = 7.73, df = 3, p = .05.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "Among studies that used the latter design, the most successful interventions addressed maladaptive social cognition."
- **What "social cognition" means here:** the paper describes the CBT core as teaching lonely people "to identify automatic negative thoughts and regard them as hypotheses to be tested rather than facts".
- **Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3865701/
- **Safe wording:** "When researchers pooled the best-controlled loneliness studies, the approach that worked best wasn't just 'go meet more people'. It was changing how lonely minds read other people: catching thoughts like 'they don't want me here' and testing them instead of believing them. It's a small set of studies, but it fits what we know about how loneliness works."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Social skills training is the best fix." In the randomized studies it had **no** effect.
  - Dropping the caveat that the winning category had **only 4** randomized studies.
  - Calling the overall effect "large". It was small (−0.198) in randomized studies.

## B6 (optional). Epley & Schroeder (2014): "Mistakenly seeking solitude"
- **Citation:** Epley, N., & Schroeder, J. (2014). Mistakenly seeking solitude. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 143*(5), 1980–1999. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037323 (PMID 25019381)
- **Design:** Nine experiments on trains, buses, in taxis and in a lab waiting room.
  - **Exp 1a:** **118** commuters at the Homewood, Illinois, Metra station were randomly told to do one of three things:
    - talk to a stranger ("connection");
    - keep to themselves ("solitude");
    - commute as normal.
    - After exclusions, **97** remained for analysis.
  - **Exp 1b:** a separate group of commuters **predicted** how each condition would feel.
  - **Exps 2a/2b:** the same design with bus commuters.
  - **Exps 3a/3b:** asked how interested people thought others would be in talking.
- **Key numbers:**
  - **Exp 1a:** connection beat solitude on positivity: t(94) = 2.49, p = .02, **d = 0.63**.
    - Productivity did not differ between conditions.
    - Conversations averaged **14.2 min** and were rated very pleasant (M = 5.1 out of 6).
  - **Exp 1b:** commuters predicted the **opposite**. Talking to a stranger was predicted to be less positive than solitude (d = 0.41).
  - **Exp 2a (buses):** connection beat solitude (d = 0.56).
  - **Exp 3a:**
    - People thought others were less interested in talking than they were themselves (d = 0.44).
    - They predicted that **fewer than 47%** of commuters would be willing to talk.
    - In reality, "all who returned our surveys reported talking with the person they attempted to connect with, and not a single person reported being rebuffed".
  - **Exp 3b:** a similar result (fewer than 45%).
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "This mistaken preference for solitude stems partly from underestimating others' interest in connecting."
- **Source:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25019381/ · author PDF: https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/jschroeder/Publications/Epley%26Schroeder2014.pdf
- **Safe wording:** "In one study, Chicago-area train commuters who were asked to chat with a stranger had a nicer ride than those told to sit alone. Yet other commuters predicted the reverse. People guessed fewer than half of strangers would want to talk. Of the commuters who mailed their surveys back, not one said they were turned down."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Everyone who tried succeeded." That is true **among surveys returned**, not of everyone.
  - "Talking to strangers cures loneliness." The study measured how pleasant one commute was, not loneliness.
  - "Introverts are the exception." Personality did **not** moderate the effect in Exp 1a.
