## A1. Nami: why she would not ask (Eps 33–37)

**CONFIRMED, in plain words:**
1. Nami and her sister Nojiko were war orphans, adopted by **Bell-mère**, a former Marine who grew tangerines (mikan) in Cocoyasi Village. They were poor but close.
2. When Nami was **10**, the fish-man pirate **Arlong** took over the island. Everyone had to pay to stay alive: 100,000 per adult, 50,000 per child, every month.
3. Bell-mère had only 100,000. She could have paid for herself by pretending she had no children. She refused to deny her daughters, paid for the two girls instead, and Arlong **shot her dead in front of them** (Ep 36).
4. Arlong saw the maps Nami drew and took her as his mapmaker. He made a deal: bring him **100 million** and he would sell her the village back. She was marked with his crew's tattoo.
5. She told her sister she would **fight alone**. Her reason: if she asked anyone for help, more people would die. (Wiki, citing ch. 79 / Ep 36.)
6. For **8 years** she stole from pirates and buried the money in the tangerine orchard. She let the village think she had sold out.
7. She used Luffy's crew the same way, then **stole their ship** and left. When they followed her home she told them they were never her crewmates and to get off the island. She even **pretended to kill Usopp**, secretly stabbing her own hand so the blood would look real (Ep 33).
8. With **93 million** saved (7 million short), a corrupt Marine captain, **Nezumi**, arrived and seized it all as "stolen goods". Arlong had sent him. Nojiko was shot in the struggle (Eps 36–37).
9. During the seizure Genzo, the village policeman, let slip that the village **had always known** why she joined Arlong. They had kept quiet so she would never feel trapped by their hopes.
10. Arlong told Nami he had broken no promise: she could just start again from zero.
11. The villagers picked up weapons to fight, knowing they would lose. Nami begged them not to go, even pulling a knife to block the road. They went.

**The scene (Ep 37) — CONFIRMED beats, in order:**
- Left alone, kneeling in the village street, Nami **stabs the Arlong tattoo on her left shoulder again and again**, shouting his name.
- **Luffy catches her arm** and stops the knife.
- Without turning round, she tells him he knows nothing about this and that she already told him to leave. She throws handfuls of dirt at him.
- He agrees he knows nothing. He stays.
- She turns, crying, and says his name and asks him to **help her**.
- He says nothing. He **puts his straw hat on her head**, walks a few steps away, and **shouts** that of course he will.
- She is stunned: she knows what that hat means to him.
- **Zoro, Usopp and Sanji are sitting waiting just ahead.** Luffy says let's go. They get up and answer as one. The four walk to Arlong Park.
- At the gate, the bounty hunters Johnny and Yosaku (beaten and bloody) hold the villagers back so the crew can fight instead. Luffy smashes through the gate.

**VERIFY by watching Ep 37:** exactly where the episode cuts (the gate-smash may fall at the end of 37 or the start of 38), and Luffy's exact pose when he shouts.

**CAUTION — version differences:**
- The old **4Kids dub cut the stabbing**. Viewers of that dub saw a different scene.
- The 2012 TV special *Episode of Nami* re-animated this scene with a **dark, stormy sky**. The **original Ep 37 has a bright blue sky** with white clouds. Choose one look on purpose.

**What the moment means (paraphrase, safe to narrate):**
- She had carried it alone for eight years because she believed asking would only get people hurt. The first time she asked, the answer was instant.
- Luffy did not ask for the reason. He had never even heard her story. He only needed her to ask.
- The hat is his greatest treasure. Putting it on her head says "I'm coming back, and you're one of us" without a speech.

**Lines often misquoted — avoid:**
- Her line is only his name and "help me". (JP gist: "Luffy… help me.") Don't add extra words about the village.
- His reply is **one shouted phrase**, "Of course I will!" (JP gist: "Of course!"). He gives no speech here.
- He does **not** say "you are my friend" in this scene. That shout comes at the **end of the fight (Ep 43)**.
- He does not *give* her the hat. He leaves it with her. She returns it after the fight (manga ch. 94; Ep 43, **VERIFY**).
- Don't say he acted "after hearing her story". He skipped it.

## A2. Robin: why she tried to give herself up (Eps 274–278)

**CONFIRMED, in plain words:**
1. Robin grew up on **Ohara**, an island of scholars. She was a lonely child, called a demon by other kids because of her powers. At **8** she passed the exam to become an archaeologist.
2. The scholars were secretly studying forbidden history. The **World Government** answered with a **Buster Call**: a fleet of warships that wipes an island off the map. Her mother, her teachers and a giant named Saul, her first friend, died. Saul's last words to her: somewhere out at sea she would find friends.
3. She escaped alone. The Government put a huge bounty (79 million) on an 8-year-old and spread the story that she was a monster.
4. For **20 years** she ran. People who took her in sold her out for the reward. The world told her, again and again, that her very existence was a crime.
5. She finally found a crew she loved: Luffy's.
6. In Water 7, the Government's secret unit **CP9** cornered her. They had permission to call a Buster Call on the Straw Hats. Her deal: she would go with them to be executed, **if her six crewmates were allowed to leave safely**. (Revealed by Iceburg to Nami; wiki cites ch. 359 / Ep 251. **VERIFY** the episode number if you cite it.)
7. So she told the crew she was leaving and let them think she had betrayed them.
8. The crew worked it out and attacked **Enies Lobby**, the Government's island courthouse, to get her back.

**The scene (Eps 274 and 278) — CONFIRMED beats:**
- **Ep 274:** Luffy has just beaten a CP9 agent (Blueno) on the **courthouse roof**. Robin, in handcuffs, appears on the balcony of the **Tower of Justice** across a huge gap with a waterfall. She shouts that she doesn't want to be saved and wants to die. One by one the rest of the crew arrive and **line up along the roof's edge**.
- **Eps 275–278:** her childhood is shown.
- **Ep 278:** Robin admits her last fear. Her enemy is the whole world. One day the crew will see her as a burden and throw her away, like everyone else did. She would rather die first.
- CP9's chief, **Spandam**, laughs, agrees she is a burden, and points up at the **World Government flag** on top of the tower: more than 170 nations stand behind it.
- Luffy looks at the flag and tells **Sogeking** to shoot it. Sogeking fires a flaming shot from his giant slingshot and **burns the flag**. Everyone on the island is horrified: the crew has just declared war on the world for her.
- Spandam screams that they can't survive with the whole world as their enemy. Luffy roars back that that's fine by him.
- Luffy yells at Robin to **say she wants to live**.
- She remembers Saul's promise. She realises nobody ever let her say what she wanted. Sobbing, she shouts that **she wants to live** and asks them to take her back out to sea.
- Luffy grins. Franky, beside her, bursts into tears. The crew gets ready to fight CP9.

**CAUTION:**
- "Sogeking" is **Usopp in a mask**. In the story Luffy and Chopper don't realise it, and Luffy calls him Sogeking ("Sniper King" in the English dub). Safe narration: "Usopp, in his Sogeking disguise".
- The flag is **burned through by a fire shot**. It is not torn down or cut.
- At this point the Buster Call on Enies Lobby has **not** been called yet. Spandam triggers it by accident later. Don't say the ships are already coming.
- The episode title's second half ("We are friends") is a title, not a line Luffy says at that moment. Translations of *nakama* vary (friends, crewmates, comrades).
- "Her existence is a sin": don't pin it on one named speaker. "The world told her" is safe.
- Spelling: **Enies Lobby**. The tower is "Tower of Justice" on the wiki and "Tower of Law" on Wikipedia.

**What the moment means (paraphrase, safe to narrate):**
- Robin wasn't refusing help out of pride. She believed that needing people makes you a burden, and burdens get abandoned.
- The crew didn't argue her out of it. They showed her the cost was already paid: they burned the flag first, then asked her to say it.
- Luffy wouldn't save her without hearing it. She had to say what she wanted out loud, for the first time in her life.

**Lines often misquoted — avoid:**
- Luffy: "Say you want to live!" (a command, shouted). Not a gentle question.
- Robin: "I want to live!" then, in the same breath, asking to be taken to sea with them. Keep any quote to those few words.
- Don't write "Luffy burns the flag" or "Usopp, as himself, shoots".

# PART B — Psychology (primary sources)

Abstracts and citation data for B1, B2 and B5 were retrieved from PubMed. Full texts for B1, B2, B3 and B4 are the authors' own accepted manuscripts. Retraction status for every DOI was checked on Crossref on 2026-11-28: none has a retraction or correction notice.

## B1. Flynn & Lake (2008): people say yes more than we expect

- **Citation:** Flynn, F. J., & Lake, V. K. B. (2008). If you need help, just ask: Underestimating compliance with direct requests for help. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 95*(1), 128–143. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.95.1.128 (PMID 18605856)
- **Name note:** Vanessa K. B. Lake is the same researcher as **Vanessa Bohns** (B3). Later papers cite this one as "Flynn & Lake (Bohns)".
- **Design:**
  - Six studies. In Studies 1 and 2, Columbia University students first guessed how many strangers they would have to ask, then went out on campus and asked.
  - Study 3 followed real charity fundraisers.
  - Studies 4–6 tested why: people asking focus on the cost of saying yes and overlook how awkward it is to say no.
- **Key numbers (all CONFIRMED in the paper text):**
  - **Study 1** (42 students completed it; about half were asked to predict first): get **5** strangers to fill in a questionnaire. Predicted they'd need to ask **20.5** people. Actually asked **10.5**.
  - **Study 2**, borrow a stranger's cell phone, **3** yeses needed: predicted **10.1**, actual **6.2**.
  - **Study 2**, get a stranger to walk you to the campus gym, **1** yes needed: predicted **7.2**, actual **2.3**.
  - In Study 2, 23 of 29 participants (79%) made the error.
  - **Study 3** (91 charity fundraisers with full data): predicted they'd need to ask **210.3** people to hit their target, actually asked **122.2**. Donations averaged **$63.80**, against a predicted $48.33.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "people underestimated by as much as 50% the likelihood that others would agree to a direct request for help"
- **Source:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18605856/ · author manuscript: https://hdl.handle.net/1813/75652
- **Limits:** small samples; university students in New York; small favours asked of strangers; Study 3 is self-report. People who found the task too scary dropped out before asking (13 of 48 in Study 2).
- **Safe wording:** "Researchers had students guess how many strangers they'd need to ask before five agreed to fill in a survey. They guessed about twenty. It took about ten."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "People overestimate by 50%." The 50% is about the chance of a yes. In asks, people expected to need roughly **double**.
  - "Everyone says yes." Roughly half the strangers said yes in these studies. The point is that it was about twice what people expected.
  - Treating it as proof for big favours or for close relationships. It tested small favours from strangers.

## B2. Zhao & Epley (2022): helpers feel better about it than we think

- **Citation:** Zhao, X., & Epley, N. (2022). Surprisingly happy to have helped: Underestimating prosociality creates a misplaced barrier to asking for help. *Psychological Science, 33*(10), 1708–1731. https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976221097615 (PMID 36067802)
- **Design:**
  - Six preregistered experiments, total **N = 2,118** US adults, using imagined scenarios, remembered requests, and live requests.
  - **Experiment 3** (the one to animate): in a free public **botanical garden** in a large US city, visitors had to ask a stranger to take their photo with an instant camera. Before asking, they predicted how the stranger would feel. Afterwards the stranger reported how they actually felt. 50 pairs.
  - Experiment 4 repeated it with friends as well as strangers (100 pairs).
- **Key numbers (Experiment 3, 0–10 scales; from the authors' accepted manuscript):**
  - Willingness to help: askers expected **7.45**, helpers reported **8.87**.
  - Helper's positive mood: expected **6.80**, actual **7.87**.
  - Helper's inconvenience: expected **3.21**, actual **0.51**.
  - The **first stranger asked said yes in 46 of 50 cases**. In the other 4 the second person did.
- **Other findings:**
  - Experiment 2 (198 people recalling real requests, mostly to friends and family): **95.4%** of the remembered requests had been agreed to. Askers still rated their helper as less willing and less happy than helpers rated themselves.
  - Experiment 4: askers underestimated **friends'** willingness too (8.68 expected vs 9.29 actual), though less than for strangers (7.51 vs 9.00).
  - A pilot (n = 75) found people were more willing to ask when they expected the helper to be willing and to feel good, and less willing when they expected the helper to feel put out.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "those needing help consistently underestimated others' willingness to help, underestimated how positively helpers would feel, and overestimated how inconvenienced helpers would feel."
- **Source:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36067802/ · https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976221097615 · authors' in-press manuscript PDF: https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/5c484e0f4aa6f839dc553c45/6230f5d585264493079e0281_Surprisingly%20Happy%20to%20Have%20Helped_Zhao%20and%20Epley_in%20press.pdf · data and materials: https://osf.io/j67c3/
- **Limits:** US adults only; **small, quick favours** (a photo, a phone); helpers' feelings are self-reported; helpers who refused the survey are not counted (4 in Experiment 3). **VERIFY** the decimals against the published version if you put digits on screen.
- **Safe wording:** "In a 2022 study, people had to ask a stranger in a public garden to take their photo. Before asking, they guessed how the stranger would feel. The strangers turned out more willing, happier to help, and far less bothered than the askers expected."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Helping makes helpers happy" as a law. The finding is a **gap between expectation and reality** for small requests.
  - "A public park" for the photo study. The paper says a botanical garden. (Experiment 1a, an imagined phone request, was run in a public park.)
  - Stretching it to life-changing favours. The paper did not test those.

## B3. Bohns (2016): the one-line review

- **Citation:** Bohns, V. K. (2016). (Mis)understanding our influence over others: A review of the underestimation-of-compliance effect. *Current Directions in Psychological Science, 25*(2), 119–123. https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721415628011
- **One-line summary:** Across studies in which participants asked **more than 14,000 strangers** for things, people underestimated how likely others were to say yes by **48% on average**, mainly because they overlook how awkward it is for someone to say no.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "on average, requesters underestimate compliance by 48%"
- **Source:** author manuscript https://hdl.handle.net/1813/74812
- **Useful extra, CONFIRMED in the text:**
  - The gap shrinks or flips when the request is made by flyer or email. Face to face is where people say yes more than expected.
  - It is larger in the US than in China.
  - Almost all targets were **strangers**. Bohns herself notes this.
- **CAUTION — an honest tension to keep:** Bohns explains the yes mostly by social pressure (it is awkward to refuse). Zhao & Epley (B2) argue helpers also genuinely *want* to help and feel good. The two papers agree on the gap and differ on the reason. Don't present "people only help because they can't say no", and don't present "helping is always pure joy" either.
- **Safe wording:** "A review of this research, covering more than fourteen thousand requests to strangers, found the same thing each time: we expect far more 'no' than we get."

## B4. Why asking is hard

**Recommended source (verified): Bohns & Flynn (2010)**
- **Citation:** Bohns, V. K., & Flynn, F. J. (2010). "Why didn't you just ask?" Underestimating the discomfort of help-seeking. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 46*(2), 402–409. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2009.12.015
- **Design:** Four studies. Study 1 asked 35 MBA peer advisors and 91 undergraduate teaching assistants to predict how many students would come to them for help over a semester, then compared that with how many did. Later studies used role-play scenarios to test the reason.
- **Key numbers (Study 1):**
  - Peer advisors expected **12.6** students to ask them for help. **7.6** did.
  - Teaching assistants expected **17.8**. **14.7** did.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "people in a position to provide help tend to underestimate the role that embarrassment plays in decisions about whether or not to ask for help."
- **Source:** author manuscript https://hdl.handle.net/1813/74811
- **Why it fits the episode:** it is the mirror image of B1. Askers underestimate how willing helpers are; helpers underestimate how hard it is to ask. Both sides are standing on a roof waiting for the other to speak first.
- **Limits:** Study 1 is a field survey with no random assignment (the authors say so), and the two sample sizes are modest.
- **Safe wording:** "The people who could help us don't realise how hard it is to ask. In one study, student advisors expected about thirteen classmates to come to them for help. Fewer than eight did."

**Also usable, already verified:** the Zhao & Epley pilot in B2 (people hold back when they expect the helper to feel inconvenienced).

**Lee (1997) — citation only, findings NOT verified**
- Lee, F. (1997). When the going gets tough, do the tough ask for help? Help seeking and power motivation in organizations. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 72*(3), 336–363. https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.1997.2746
- I could not open the abstract or the paper. Later papers describe its argument as: asking for help can signal incompetence and dependence, so people who care about status avoid it.
- **Recommendation:** don't put specific findings from this paper in the script. If you want the idea, say it in general terms and don't attach numbers.
- **Safe wording (general, no citation needed on screen):** "Asking can feel like admitting you can't cope."

**Brooks, Gino & Schweitzer (2015) — recommend NOT using**
- Brooks, A. W., Gino, F., & Schweitzer, M. E. (2015). Smart people ask for (my) advice: Seeking advice boosts perceptions of competence. *Management Science, 61*(6), 1421–1435. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2014.2054
- **Status, checked 2026-11-28:** no retraction or correction on Crossref or the RePEc record.
- **Integrity context:** Francesca Gino is a co-author, and several of her other papers have been retracted over data problems. On the Many Co-Authors project (paper #68) both co-authors report that Gino was involved in data collection for **Study 4**; for the other seven studies they answered "don't know"; and neither has data that would let the results be reproduced. https://manycoauthors.org/gino/68
- **Recommendation:** leave it out. It is about asking for *advice* at work, not help in general, and B1, B2 and B4 already cover the point with cleaner sources.
- **CAUTION:** don't say it was retracted. It wasn't.

## B5. Does helping feel good for the helper?

**Dunn, Aknin & Norton (2008)**
- **Citation:** Dunn, E. W., Aknin, L. B., & Norton, M. I. (2008). Spending money on others promotes happiness. *Science, 319*(5870), 1687–1688. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1150952 (PMID 18356530)
- **Design:** Three parts. A national US survey linking how people spend to how happy they are. A small study of employees before and after a bonus. One experiment: people on a Canadian university campus were handed **$5 or $20** and randomly told to spend it that day on themselves or on someone else; happiness was measured that evening.
- **Key numbers:**
  - The experiment had **46 participants** across four groups, and its key test was **p = .042** (as reported by the 2022 replication paper below).
  - Secondary summaries give 632 people for the survey and 16 employees for the bonus study. I could not open the *Science* text to confirm, so **don't narrate those two numbers**.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "participants who were randomly assigned to spend money on others experienced greater happiness than those assigned to spend money on themselves."
- **Source:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18356530/

**Replication status — CONFIRMED**
- **Aknin, L. B., Dunn, E. W., Proulx, J., Lok, I., & Norton, M. I. (2020).** Does spending money on others promote happiness?: A registered replication report. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 119*(2), e15–e26. https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000191 (PMID 32250135)
  - Experiment 1: **712** students bought a small gift bag for themselves or for a sick child. The giving group was happier.
  - Experiment 2: **1,950** adults recalled past spending. **No difference.**
  - Experiment 3: **5,199** adults, with a more engaging version. Giving group happier, "but differences were small".
  - Authors' own conclusion: the effect is real, and its size depends on how the study is run.
- **Kim, G., Adams, I., Diaw, M., Celly, M., Nelson, L. D., & Jung, M. H. (2022).** Prosocial spending encourages happiness: A replication of the only experiment reported in Dunn, Aknin, and Norton (2008). *PLOS ONE, 17*(9), e0272434. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272434 (open access, PMC9451075)
  - An independent close replication, N = 133, with $10. On the original happiness measure: **no significant difference**. On a simpler "how happy were you right after spending" question: giving group happier (4.36 vs 4.04 on a 5-point scale).
  - The authors call the result mixed.
- **Cross-cultural (optional):** Aknin, L. B., Barrington-Leigh, C. P., Dunn, E. W., Helliwell, J. F., Burns, J., Biswas-Diener, R., Kemeza, I., Nyende, P., Ashton-James, C. E., & Norton, M. I. (2013). Prosocial spending and well-being: Cross-cultural evidence for a psychological universal. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 104*(4), 635–652. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031578 (PMID 23421360). Survey data from **136 countries** link giving with greater happiness; experiments in Canada, Uganda, India and South Africa point the same way. The survey part is correlational.
- **Fit for this episode:** these studies are about **spending money on others**, which is not the same as being asked for help. **B2 is the direct evidence** that the person you ask ends up feeling good. Use B5 as a short supporting beat at most.
- **Safe wording:** "In experiments, people given a little money to spend on someone else ended the day a bit happier than people told to spend it on themselves. Larger repeat studies found the effect is real but small."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Giving makes you much happier." The well-powered estimates are small.
  - Quoting the 2008 experiment as strong proof. It had 46 people.
  - "Proven in 136 countries." That part is a survey correlation.

## B6. Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton (2010) — already verified

See `../anime-ep02-naruto-loneliness/RESEARCH.md`, section B3. Nothing re-checked here.
- Holt-Lunstad, J., Smith, T. B., & Layton, J. B. (2010). Social relationships and mortality risk: A meta-analytic review. *PLoS Medicine, 7*(7), e1000316. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316
- 148 studies, 308,849 participants, OR = 1.50 (95% CI 1.42–1.59). Observational.
- **Safe wording (same as Ep 02):** "A review of 148 studies and over 300,000 people found that those with stronger social ties had about 50% higher odds of still being alive at follow-up. That's a correlation, not proof of cause."
- Still **not** "lonely people are 50% more likely to die", and the "15 cigarettes" line is not in this paper.

## How the science lines up with the two scenes (for the script)

| Scene beat | Study | The honest version of the link |
|---|---|---|
| Nami expects asking to cost the people she asks | B2 pilot, B2 Exp 3 | We hold back when we picture the helper being put out. Helpers report being far less bothered than we picture. |

| The crew waits on the roof for her to say it | B4 | People who could help don't realise how hard asking is, so they wait for a request that doesn't come. |
| Franky cries; Luffy grins | B2, B5 | Helpers tend to feel good about helping, more than the asker expects. Effects on happiness are modest. |
| "You don't have to carry it alone" | B6 | Stronger social ties go with longer life. Correlation only. |

**Limits to say out loud at least once:** these studies used everyday favours, mostly in the US, often with strangers. Nobody has run an experiment on eight-year secrets or death sentences. The research shows our fear of asking is **miscalibrated**; it does not promise every person will say yes.
