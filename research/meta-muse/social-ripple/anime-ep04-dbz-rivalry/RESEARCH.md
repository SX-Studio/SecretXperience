## A1. The prince and the "low-class warrior": the first fight (Eps 28–35)

**CONFIRMED, in plain words:**
1. Vegeta is the **prince of the Saiyans**, a nearly extinct warrior race. Goku is a Saiyan too, sent to Earth as a baby because his power at birth was so low. Vegeta calls him by his Saiyan name, Kakarot.
2. Before they fight (Ep 30), Vegeta mocks Goku as a **low-class** soldier. Goku answers that with enough training a low-class warrior can pass an **elite**. The whole rivalry grows from that one exchange.
3. One-on-one, Vegeta is stronger. Goku only keeps up by using a technique that multiplies his power at the cost of tearing up his own body, and he feels **excited rather than worried** to meet someone stronger (wiki summary of Ep 30).
4. Goku wins a beam struggle (Ep 31). Vegeta answers by making an artificial moon and turning into a giant ape, and **crushes Goku's legs** (Ep 32). Goku cannot fight any more.
5. It takes everyone to stop him: Yajirobe cuts off his tail (Ep 33), Krillin and Gohan hit him with the Spirit Bomb (Ep 34), and Gohan, turned into an ape himself, falls on him (Ep 35).
6. Vegeta, barely alive, crawls to his space pod. **Krillin picks up Yajirobe's sword to finish him. Goku, lying broken on the ground, asks Krillin by telepathy to let him go.** Krillin stops. Vegeta leaves, swearing revenge.

**CAUTION — version difference on Goku's reason (Ep 35):**
- **Japanese anime, manga (ch. 241) and Kai:** Goku says he wants to fight Vegeta again and beat him himself. It is a selfish wish, and he says so.
- **Older English dubs (1997 and 2005):** changed to mercy. Goku hopes kindness will plant something good in him.
- Both versions include the idea that it would be a **waste** to let the strongest opponent he has ever met die.
- For this episode's theme the original reason is the stronger one: Goku meets someone better than him and his first wish is "again".

**CAUTION — who won:** do not say "Goku beat Vegeta". The wiki's timeline calls it a draw. Vegeta himself later calls it his first great humiliation (Ep 230), which is how *he* scored it.

## A2. The scouter line (Ep 28)

**CONFIRMED:**
- A **scouter** is the eyepiece Frieza's soldiers wear. It reads an opponent's "power level" as a number.
- Goku has just arrived and seen his dead friends. He powers up in anger. Nappa asks Vegeta what the scouter says. **Vegeta** answers, takes the scouter off and **crushes it in his fist**.
- **1997 edited English dub (Ocean cast, Vegeta voiced by Brian Drummond; edited episode 21, "The Return of Goku", first aired 19 Apr 1997):** "It's over 9000!" Nappa repeats the number in disbelief.
- **Japanese anime and manga:** "over 8,000" (*hassen ijō da*). The manga chapter is ch. 224, "The Quiet Wrath of Son Goku".
- **Funimation's 2005 uncut redub (Ep 28, "Goku's Arrival", Christopher Sabat):** kept 9000.
- **Kai English dub:** the TV broadcast version says 9000; the home-video and Toonami version says 8000.
- The internet meme dates from a YouTube upload on 17 Oct 2006 (Wikipedia and the wiki agree).

**CAUTION:**
- "Why 9000?" Wikipedia reports a guidebook's explanation that 9000 fit the mouth movements better, and also reports a writer who calls it a plain mistranslation. **Do not state a reason as fact.**
- The meme clip repeats the shout several times. In the episode he says it once.
- He crushes the scouter **in his hand** after taking it off. It does not explode on his face.

**Why it fits the episode (our framing, not a source):** the scouter is a machine that turns a person into a number you can compare. The first thing Vegeta does when the number is higher than he expected is break the machine.

## A3. Namek: Vegeta's death (Ep 86, "The End of Vegeta")

**CONFIRMED, in plain words:**
- Frieza, the tyrant Vegeta served all his life, has beaten Vegeta nearly to death. Goku arrives and calmly holds his own.
- Vegeta, lying broken, laughs at Frieza and says Goku is the **Super Saiyan** of legend, the warrior Frieza fears. Frieza fires a beam **through Vegeta's heart**.
- Dying, and **in tears**, Vegeta tells Goku the truth: Frieza ruled the Saiyans, took Vegeta from his father as a boy, and then destroyed their home planet and almost all of their people.
- He **begs Goku to defeat Frieza**, as a Saiyan. Then he dies.
- Goku buries him and takes on his request.

**CAUTION — version differences:**
- English dub: he also begs Goku to stop being merciful, and to not let Frieza turn anyone else into what Vegeta became.
- Manga (ch. 308): shorter. He says Goku could become a Super Saiyan if he were more ruthless, tells him how the planet was destroyed, and dies. Goku asks for "some of his pride" as he buries him.
- The childhood flashback is anime-only.

**What it means for our theme:** the first time Vegeta asks Goku for anything, it is to do the thing Vegeta believed was *his* birthright. He hands his life's goal to his rival because he is dying, and he cries doing it.

## A4. Super Saiyan: Goku first, then Vegeta (Eps 95 and 129)

**CONFIRMED:**
- **Goku becomes a Super Saiyan in Ep 95, "Transformed at Last".** Trigger: Frieza kills Krillin in front of him. Hair turns gold, eyes turn blue-green, a golden aura appears.
- **Vegeta reveals he can do it in Ep 129, "Upgrade to Super Saiyan"**, during the android fight, more than thirty episodes later. He arrives, saves Goku (saying nobody gets to kill Kakarot but him), and transforms in front of everyone.
- **His own account (English dub of Ep 129, per the wiki's summary):** his motivation was different from Goku's; he wanted to be the very best. He trained alone at 450 times normal gravity, where the only thing holding him together was the wish to be stronger than Kakarot. During a meteor storm in space he hit his limit, something in him snapped, and he stopped caring whether he lived or became a Super Saiyan at all. Then it happened.
- **Kanzenshuu's synopsis of the Japanese episode:** he awakened "through his anger at himself for losing to Goku".
- His **first** transformation happened off-screen, between sagas. The anime shows it as a flashback inside this episode.

**CAUTION — the "pure" line:**
- **Manga (ch. 343):** Krillin says he thought you needed a pure heart. Vegeta says his heart is pure: pure **evil**. He then says it awakened from rage at himself.
- **Kai:** the line is there.
- **Original anime Ep 129:** the Dragon Ball Wiki says the anime leaves the claim out and uses the training flashback. **VERIFY** by ear if you want the line.
- Safe narration for all versions: "Goku changed out of grief for a friend. Vegeta changed out of fury at himself for being second."

**In Ep 280 he tells it again** (English dub): seeing Goku ahead of him "tore me apart", and he lived every day for the single purpose of surpassing him.

## A5. Majin Vegeta and the sacrifice (Eps 228–231, 237)

**CONFIRMED, in plain words:**
1. Seven years of peace have passed. Vegeta has a wife (Bulma) and a son (Trunks). Goku, dead for those seven years, is back for a single day.
2. The wizard **Babidi** can enslave anyone with evil in their heart and push their power past its limit. His spell lands on Vegeta (Ep 228). A black "**M**" appears on Vegeta's forehead.
3. Vegeta fires into the tournament crowd, killing spectators, to force Goku to fight him (Eps 228–229).
4. **Goku works out that Vegeta let it happen on purpose** (Ep 229). Vegeta does not deny it.
5. **Reason one (Ep 229):** Goku is only back for one day and things keep getting in the way. And he, a prince, has had to watch a low-class warrior pass him and even **save his life**, which he says he cannot forgive.
6. Babidi orders him to kill someone else. **Vegeta refuses.** He says the wizard may have his mind and body but not his pride. He only wants Goku.
7. **Reason two (Ep 231, "Magic Ball of Buu"):** Goku asks why he, of all people, accepted help. Vegeta says he wanted the evil in him woken up again. He used to be a perfect, cold fighter. Over the years he became "one of you": he settled down, had a family, even grew fond of them, and started to think Earth was a nice place. He wanted to be free of those attachments. Goku does not believe he means it.
8. Their fight releases the monster Majin Buu. Vegeta knocks Goku out and goes to face Buu alone.
9. **Ep 237, "Final Atonement":** he tells his son Trunks to take care of his mother, says he has never held him since he was a baby, and **hugs him** for the first time. Then he knocks both boys out so they can be carried to safety.
10. He asks Piccolo whether he will meet Goku in the afterlife. Piccolo tells him no: he has killed too many people. Vegeta accepts it.
11. He thinks a farewell to **Bulma, Trunks, and Kakarot**, and blows himself up to destroy Buu. (Technique name: Final Explosion.)
12. Buu survives. The sacrifice fails.

**CAUTION:**
- "On equal terms" is our summary. What the sources say: the spell raises power, Goku realises Vegeta allowed it knowing that, and Vegeta's stated reasons are the two above. Do not put the words "equal terms" in his mouth.
- **The hug:** Trunks is in **Super Saiyan form (blond)** in the frame I viewed, and so is Vegeta.
- **Line to Trunks:** English dub, "you've made me proud". Manga, "be well". Japanese anime line: **VERIFY**.
- **Final thought:** English dub, he does it "for" Bulma, Trunks and "even" Kakarot. Manga, it is a farewell to the three. Same three names either way.
- Piccolo says this is the first time Vegeta has fought for someone other than himself. The wiki notes that is arguable.
- Vegeta is **under the spell but not obedient**. Do not say Babidi "controls" him.

**Lines often misquoted — avoid:**
- Do not say he was "tricked" or "possessed against his will". He chose it.
- Do not say he killed the crowd because Babidi ordered it. He did it to provoke Goku.

## A6. The monologue (Ep 280, "Vegeta's Respect")

**Where we are (CONFIRMED):** the final battle, on the Sacred World of the Kais. Kid Buu, the last and wildest form of the monster, is fighting **Goku in his Super Saiyan 3 form**. Vegeta has just tried to fight Kid Buu himself and could not land a punch. Goku saved him and went back in.

**What happens (CONFIRMED):** Vegeta **watches from a distance** and thinks back over their whole history. It is an inner monologue. The flashbacks were newly drawn for this episode.

**Substance of what he admits (English Funimation dub, from the wiki's quote page; paraphrased):**
1. Goku has always been like this: ready for a challenge even when it is bigger than he is.
2. On Namek, seeing Goku so far ahead tore him apart. How could a low-class soldier do so easily what he had struggled for all his life?
3. He became a Super Saiyan too, after living every day to surpass Goku. And still Goku stayed ahead.
4. He first thought it was Goku's loved ones that drove him. But then Vegeta had a family of his own, and his power did not rise.
5. He himself fought for the pleasure of it and spared no one.
6. Goku showed mercy to everyone, even to him. Goku **never fought to kill or for revenge, only to test his limits and become the strongest he could be**.
7. That makes him angry. Then: maybe his anger is what kept him from seeing the truth.
8. He sees it now. Kakarot is better than him. **Closing words in this dub: "You are the best."**

**Japanese anime and manga (ch. 510), from the wiki's chapter summary and Kanzenshuu:** same shape, with one difference worth knowing. He says Goku does not fight to *win*; he fights so that he will **not lose**, always pushing past his own limits, which is why he never cared about killing his opponents. He calls him a gentle Saiyan who loves fighting. He ends by cheering him on: do your best, Kakarot, **"you are No. 1"**.

**Kai English dub (2018):** adds a line that is not in the other versions, roughly: I fought to push others down, you fight to push yourself. It ends "Hang in there, Kakarot! **You are number one!**"

**CAUTION:**
- **Which closing line?** "You are the best" (2003 dub of Ep 280) or "You are number one" (Japanese, manga, Kai dub). Fans know both. If the poster card says "YOU ARE NUMBER ONE", tag it as the Japanese/Kai line, or say "in the original".
- The "push others down / push yourself" sentence is **Kai dub only**. It is perfect for our theme, but do not present it as a line from Ep 280.
- The dub says "the best", the title says "No. 1". Do not write "Goku, you're number one" as the 2003 dub line.
- He does **not** say this out loud, and Goku does not react to it.
- Right after (Ep 281) he volunteers to hold Kid Buu off alone for one minute so Goku can gather energy, knowing he may die. The admission is followed by an act.

**What the moment means (paraphrase, safe to narrate):**
- For the whole series Vegeta has kept score against one person. Here he keeps score one last time, loses, and for the first time it does not break him.
- What he notices is not that Goku is stronger. It is that Goku was never measuring himself against Vegeta at all.

## A7. How long the rivalry runs

**CONFIRMED numbers:**
- First face-to-face: Ep 28. Fight begins: **Ep 30**. Monologue: **Ep 280**. That is **250 episodes** from the first punch to "you are No. 1".
- The rematch (Ep 230) begins exactly 200 episodes after the first fight (wiki trivia; the arithmetic checks).
- **Broadcast time in Japan:** 13 Dec 1989 (Ep 30) to 18 Oct 1995 (Ep 280): just under **six years** of weekly episodes.
- **Story time:** the wiki's timeline, which follows the official guidebooks, puts the first fight on 3 November of Age 762 and the Buu saga in May of Age 774. That is about **eleven and a half years**. **CAUTION:** these dates come from guidebooks, not from the show itself. Safe narration: "more than a decade in the story".

**CAUTION:** do not say "291 episodes of rivalry" (that is the whole series) or "30 years" (that is the real-world age of the franchise, and I did not check it).

## B1. Festinger (1954): the theory

- **Citation:** Festinger, L. (1954). A theory of social comparison processes. *Human Relations, 7*(2), 117–140. https://doi.org/10.1177/001872675400700202
- **What it is:** a **theory paper**. It lays out nine numbered hypotheses with corollaries and points to earlier experiments. It is not itself an experiment. Say "Festinger proposed", never "Festinger showed".
- **What it claims (CONFIRMED in the text):**
  - **Hypothesis I:** people have a drive to evaluate their opinions and abilities.
  - **Hypothesis II:** when there is no objective, non-social yardstick, people evaluate themselves by comparison with other people.
  - **Hypothesis III:** the tendency to compare with a particular person **falls as the gap grows**. We compare with people close to us. His example: a college student does not measure himself against someone far above or far below.
  - **Hypothesis IV:** for abilities (unlike opinions) there is a "unidirectional drive upward". Because of it, a person aims at a point slightly better than their own performance or than the people they compare with.
  - **Hypothesis VI:** stopping comparison with someone can come with hostility or putting them down.
- **Verbatim (Hypothesis I):** "There exists, in the human organism, a drive to evaluate his opinions and his abilities."
- **A limit Festinger states himself:** the push to do better and better holds "in Western culture, at any rate", and he leaves open whether it is cultural.
- **Source:** reading copy at humanscience.org (retyped from the journal; wording matches the standard quotations). **VERIFY** against the publisher's PDF if you put a quote on screen.
- **Safe wording:** "In 1954 the psychologist Leon Festinger proposed that we have a built-in need to know how good we are, and that when there's no ruler to measure with, we use other people. And not just anyone: people close to our own level."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Festinger proved…" It is a theory.
  - "We compare ourselves with everyone." His claim is the opposite: with **similar** others.
  - "Upward and downward comparison" as Festinger's terms. Those labels came later. He wrote of a drive upward for abilities.
  - "Comparison is bad for you." The paper does not say that.

## B2. Kilduff, Elfenbein & Staw (2010): where rivalry comes from

- **Citation:** Kilduff, G. J., Elfenbein, H. A., & Staw, B. M. (2010). The psychology of rivalry: A relationally dependent analysis of competition. *Academy of Management Journal, 53*(5), 943–969. https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2010.54533171
- **Design:**
  - Setting: NCAA Division I men's basketball, the six major conferences, 2005–06 season.
  - Student sportswriters at 73 universities rated how much they saw each other team in their conference as a rival (0–10). 421 responded; after quality checks, **362 raters at 71 universities**. Ratings from 134 players on 11 teams agreed closely with the sportswriters (r = .89).
  - Rivalry ratings were then matched to team histories and to box scores from **563 conference games**.
- **Key numbers (all CONFIRMED in the paper text):**
  - **50.4%** of the variation in rivalry belonged to the **specific pair** of teams. Only 4.6% was about the team doing the rating and 26.2% about the team being rated.
  - Rivalry was stronger when teams were **geographically closer**, **more similar** in historic status, had **played each other more often**, and had been **more evenly matched** (head-to-head record closer to 50–50).
  - Long-run evenness predicted rivalry better than recent form.
  - In games between stronger rivals, defence was a little better: one standard deviation more rivalry went with **1.25 fewer points per 100 possessions (1.19%)** and **0.33 more blocked shots (4.47%)**. Steals: no significant link. Free-throw accuracy: no link, which the authors use to argue against "choking".
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "teams' similarity and interaction histories systematically predict rivalry, and that rivalry may affect team members' motivation and performance."
- **Source:** author-hosted PDF, https://web-docs.stern.nyu.edu/management/Kilduff/KilduffEtAl%20AMJ%202010.pdf
- **Limits:** one sport, one season, **teams not individuals**; rivalry rated by student journalists; correlational. **Effort was inferred from defensive statistics, not measured.** The authors themselves write "may affect" and call the support "some".
- **Safe wording:** "When researchers mapped rivalries in US college basketball, they found a rival isn't just any strong opponent. Rivalry was strongest between teams that were similar, had met again and again, and were evenly matched."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Rivalry makes teams try harder — proven." The effort link is a small, indirect, team-level correlation.
  - "Your rival is simply the best team around." Half the variation belonged to the specific pair, not to how good either team was.
  - Dropping geography. Closeness on the map was the single strongest predictor.

## B3. Kilduff (2014): the runners

- **Citation:** Kilduff, G. J. (2014). Driven to win: Rivalry, motivation, and performance. *Social Psychological and Personality Science, 5*(8), 944–952. https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550614539770
- **What I could open:** the **abstract** (Crossref), the publisher's own press release (SAGE, via EurekAlert), and two science-news reports. **I could not open the paper itself** (publisher page blocked; no repository copy found).
- **Design (abstract, CONFIRMED):** two studies of adults from the general population, then a large archival study of long-distance running. Rivals were not self-declared in the race data; they were identified from demographics and prior race results.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "runners ran faster in races featuring their rivals"
- **Details from secondary reports (not checked against the paper):**
  - SAGE press release: a US running club, races over **six years**, distances 3 to 21 km, **most were 5K**. Rival pairs were identified from the first three years (similar age and gender, many shared races, close finish times) and performance was examined in the next three.
  - Science News and the APS news site: runners were about **4.92 seconds per kilometre** faster when at least one rival was in the race, roughly **25 seconds over a 5K**.
  - **CAUTION:** the press release says **184 races**; the APS report says **82 runners in 112 races**. I could not resolve this.
  - Science News also reports an online study with 147 participants who recalled a rival or a non-rival competitor.
- **Limits:**
  - The race study is **correlational**. A psychologist quoted in Science News said it cannot show rivalry *causes* the faster time.
  - "Rivals" were inferred by a formula, not confirmed by the runners.
  - One amateur club of keen racers. The first two studies rely on people's memories of past contests.
- **Safe wording:** "In a study of amateur runners, people ran a little faster in races where a rival was also running: by press accounts of the paper, about five seconds per kilometre. That's a link, not proof of cause."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Rivals make you 5 seconds faster" with no unit. It is per **kilometre**.
  - "25 seconds faster" as the headline finding. That is the per-kilometre figure multiplied out for a 5K.
  - "Runners named their rivals." In the race data they did not.
- **VERIFY before digits go on screen:** the exact figure, the number of runners and races.

## B4. The cost side: Kilduff, Galinsky, Gallo & Reade (2016)

- **Citation:** Kilduff, G. J., Galinsky, A. D., Gallo, E., & Reade, J. J. (2016). Whatever it takes to win: Rivalry increases unethical behavior. *Academy of Management Journal, 59*(5), 1508–1534. https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2014.0545
- **Design and key numbers (all CONFIRMED in the accepted manuscript):**
  - **Study 1, archival:** **2,788 matches** in Serie A, Italy's top football league, 2002–2009. "Rivalry" = two teams from the **same city**. Rival matches averaged **6.03 yellow cards against 4.25**. Red cards 0.50 against 0.33 (borderline alone, p = .057; significant once controls were added). Held after controlling for league position, recent form and margin of victory.
  - **Study 2, experiment:** **70** Ohio State students were paired online with a (fictional) student from Michigan, their arch-rival, or from one of two other universities. They could earn more by sending their partner a false message. **50.0%** lied to the rival against **12.2%** to the others.
  - **Study 3, experiment:** **101** online adults, negotiation task. **56.4%** used some form of deception in the rivalry condition against **32.7%**.
  - **Study 4, experiment:** **243** online adults recalled a personal rival, a non-rival competitor, or an acquaintance, then imagined negotiating with them. Deception: **46.8%, 24.7%, 19.3%**.
  - **Mechanism (Study 4):** rivalry raised how much people's **self-worth and status** hung on the result, which strengthened a focus on out-performing the other person, which predicted the unethical choice.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "rivalry heightens the psychological stakes of competition (by increasing actors' contingency of self-worth and status concerns)"
- **Source:** accepted manuscript, https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/47395/ (page numbers differ from the journal).
- **Limits (the authors list most of these):**
  - Study 1: same-city is a rough stand-in for rivalry; cards are a referee's judgment and are sometimes given for non-cheating offences; referees may be stricter in derbies.
  - Study 2 is small. Studies 3 and 4 are online, and Study 4 is **hypothetical** (what people say they would write).
  - "Unethical" here means fouls and lying in a game for small money. Nothing violent.
- **Safe wording:** "The same research group found the other side. In seven seasons of Italian football, matches between same-city rivals drew more yellow cards. And in an experiment, students were about four times as likely to lie to someone from their rival university."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Rivalry makes people cheat" as a law. In every experiment a large share of people facing a rival did **not** deceive.
  - "Nine times more likely." The paper's 9.26 is an odds ratio from a small sample. The plain percentages are 50% against 12%.
  - Treating football cards as proof of cheating.

**Worth adding — Chai, Converse, Garcia & Chen (2026): two kinds of rivalry**
- **Citation:** Chai, V. E., Converse, B. A., Garcia, S. M., & Chen, P. (2026). Amicable rivalries and hostile rivalries: Divergent profiles of motivation and unethical conduct. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 111*(7), 848–860. https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0001353 (PMID 41678231)
- **Findings (abstract, from PubMed; I did not open the full text, so no sample sizes):** people can name both warm ("amicable") and cold ("hostile") rivalries in their lives. Both raised motivation compared with ordinary competition. **Only hostile rivalries raised unethical intentions.** The difference was partly explained by focusing on the **outcome of winning** versus the **process of competing**. Asking people to reflect on what they get from the process of competing against a hostile rival **reduced** unethical intentions.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "stronger motivation without increased unethical intentions"
- **Limits:** brand new, one paper, measures **intentions** not behaviour. No independent replication yet.
- **Safe wording:** "A 2026 study suggests the push and the poison can be separated. Friendly rivalries gave people the motivation without the urge to cheat. What mattered was whether they cared about the contest itself, or only about winning it."

## B5. Benign and malicious envy: van de Ven, Zeelenberg & Pieters

**2009 — two kinds of envy**
- **Citation:** van de Ven, N., Zeelenberg, M., & Pieters, R. (2009). Leveling up and down: The experiences of benign and malicious envy. *Emotion, 9*(3), 419–429. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015669 (PMID 19485619)
- **Design (abstract):** Study 1 asked Dutch participants to recall an experience of one of the two envy types and describe what it was like. Dutch has two words: ***benijden*** (benign) and ***afgunst*** (malicious). Studies 2 and 3 used samples from the **United States** and **Spain**, where one word covers both, and used a statistical clustering method to see whether two kinds of experience still appear. They did.
- **Finding:** benign envy came with a wish to **move up**; malicious envy with a wish to **pull the other person down**.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "the experience of benign envy leads to a moving-up motivation aimed at improving one's own position"
- **Not opened:** the full text, so no sample sizes here. The 2011 paper adds that malicious envy goes with feeling the other person's advantage is **undeserved**, benign with feeling it is deserved.

**2011 — envy against admiration**
- **Citation:** van de Ven, N., Zeelenberg, M., & Pieters, R. (2011). Why envy outperforms admiration. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 37*(6), 784–795. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211400421 (PMID 21383070)
- **Design and key numbers (CONFIRMED in the paper text):**
  - **Study 1:** **17** students thought of someone better than them. Only benign envy went with planning more study hours.
  - **Study 2:** **86** participants recalled benign envy, malicious envy, admiration, or nothing, then did an 18-item word-association test. Benign envy group **11.38** correct; admiration 9.82; malicious envy 8.48; control 9.33.
  - **Study 3:** **96** Tilburg students imagined feeling one of the emotions toward a star fellow student. Benign envy **10.21**; the other three groups between 8.54 and 8.75. The benign-envy group also kept working longer.
  - **Study 4:** **34** students. Those first led to believe people **can change** felt more benign envy toward a star student and planned about **7 more study hours**; those led to believe change is hard felt more admiration and planned almost no extra (0.41).
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "benign envy, but not the emotions of admiration or malicious envy, motivates people to improve themselves"
- **Source:** https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu (publisher's version).
- **Limits:**
  - **Small groups** (about 17 to 24 people per condition), all Dutch students.
  - Emotions were recalled or imagined. The task was a short word test. Study 4's outcome is planned hours, not performance.
  - I found **no retraction and no large preregistered replication** of the performance effect (one quick search; not exhaustive). Treat it as suggestive.
  - Later work complicates "benign = good": Lange, Paulhus & Crusius (2018, *PSPB*, https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167217746340; abstract from PubMed) found benign envy linked to manipulative behaviour, and concluded that both forms "can both be malevolent".
- **Supporting, larger:** Lange & Crusius (2015, *PSPB, 41*(2), 284–294, https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167214564959; abstract from PubMed). Four studies, total N = 1,094. People prone to benign envy set higher goals and **ran a marathon faster**; people prone to malicious envy tended to give up on their race goal. Correlational.
- **Safe wording:** "Dutch researchers describe two kinds of envy. One says 'I want to get up there too.' The other says 'I want them brought down.' In small experiments with students, only the first kind made people work harder. Admiring someone felt nicer, and changed nothing."
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Envy is good for you." Only one kind, in small studies, on short tasks.
  - "Admiration is useless." Study 4 says admiration appears when catching up seems impossible. That is a finding about when, not a verdict.
  - Presenting *benijden* and *afgunst* as proof that the two envies are universal. The US and Spanish samples support it, but this is one research group.

## B6. Looking up: who inspires and who deflates

**Lockwood & Kunda (1997)**
- **Citation:** Lockwood, P., & Kunda, Z. (1997). Superstars and me: Predicting the impact of role models on the self. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 73*(1), 91–103. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.73.1.91
- **What I opened:** the abstract (via a research index), and Lockwood's own 1998 doctoral thesis (University of Waterloo repository), which describes the 1997 studies. **Not the article itself**, so no sample sizes.
- **Findings (abstract, CONFIRMED):**
  - A star only affects how you see yourself when their field is **relevant** to you.
  - A relevant star **inspires** when their success looks **attainable**: you still have time to get there, or you believe ability can grow.
  - A relevant star **deflates**, "if anything", when their success looks **unattainable**: you have already missed the chance, or you believe ability is fixed.
- **Detail from the thesis:** future teachers were inspired by an outstanding teacher and unaffected by an outstanding accountant.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "Relevant superstars provoke self-enhancement and inspiration when their success seems attainable but self-deflation when it seems unattainable."
- **Limits:** university students; self-ratings taken minutes after reading about a star; the deflation result is hedged by the authors' own "if anything".
- **Safe wording:** "Whether someone better than you lifts you or flattens you depends on two things: is it your field, and does catching up feel possible?"
- **Common misreports:** "Role models always motivate." "Superstars make us feel worse." Both drop the condition.

**Gerber, Wheeler & Suls (2018) — the big picture (recommended addition)**
- **Citation:** Gerber, J. P., Wheeler, L., & Suls, J. (2018). A social comparison theory meta-analysis 60+ years on. *Psychological Bulletin, 144*(2), 177–197. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000127 (PMID 29144145)
- **Design:** meta-analyses of more than sixty years of social comparison experiments. Abstract from PubMed; full text not opened.
- **Findings (abstract):**
  - Given a choice of looking up or down, people showed a **strong preference for looking up**, at someone better. Even under threat, looking down was not the dominant choice.
  - The usual effect of a comparison was **contrast**: after looking at someone better, people rated themselves **lower** and felt worse.
  - The authors name the puzzle themselves: why people choose to look up when the likely result is feeling smaller.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "contrast was, by far, the dominant response to social comparison"
- **Limits:** mostly short laboratory studies; the authors note few studies had a no-comparison control group.
- **Safe wording:** "A review of sixty years of experiments found two things. Given the choice, we look up, at someone doing better. And looking up usually leaves us feeling smaller."
- **Why it fits:** it is the most direct answer to the viewer's question that the research offers, and it is honest that nobody has fully explained it.

## B7. Comparing with your own past self

**Albert (1977) — the theory**
- **Citation:** Albert, S. (1977). Temporal comparison theory. *Psychological Review, 84*(6), 485–503. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.84.6.485
- **Status:** citation confirmed. **I could not open the abstract or the text.** Later papers describe it as a theory paper that rewrites Festinger's propositions for one person at two points in time. A 2021 review summarises Albert as proposing that people prefer comparisons with their **recent** past.
- **Recommendation:** name it at most as "the idea goes back to 1977". Do not attach findings to it. Like Festinger, it is a theory, not an experiment.

**Wilson & Ross (2000, 2001) — what people actually do**
- **Citations:**
  - Wilson, A. E., & Ross, M. (2000). The frequency of temporal-self and social comparisons in people's personal appraisals. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78*(5), 928–942. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.5.928 (PMID 10821199)
  - Wilson, A. E., & Ross, M. (2001). From chump to champ: People's appraisals of their earlier and present selves. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 80*(4), 572–584. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.80.4.572 (PMID 11316222)
- **Findings (abstracts, from PubMed):**
  - 2000: when describing themselves, people mentioned comparisons with their past selves **as often as or more often than** comparisons with other people. They leaned on the past when they wanted to **feel good**, and on other people when they wanted to judge themselves **accurately**.
  - 2001: people rate their distant past selves lower than their present selves, and one study found this **even when measurements taken at the time showed no real improvement**.
- **Verbatim (2000 abstract):** "Temporal-past comparisons are gratifying, because they tend to indicate improvement over time."
- **The honest reading:** comparing with your past self feels good partly because we quietly **mark the old self down**. It is a comfort and a bias at once.

**Gürel, Brummelman, Sedikides & Overbeek (2020) — the best experiment**
- **Citation:** Gürel, Ç., Brummelman, E., Sedikides, C., & Overbeek, G. (2020). Better than my past self: Temporal comparison raises children's pride without triggering superiority goals. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 149*(8), 1554–1566. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000733 (PMID 31944812)
- **Design (abstract, from PubMed):** **583 children and teenagers** (average age 11.65; groups aged 8–10, 11–13 and 14–16) were randomly assigned to compare themselves with **other people** (as better or worse), with their **own past self** (as better or worse), or to make no comparison.
- **Findings:** seeing yourself as better than others and seeing yourself as better than your past self **both produced pride**. Only the comparison with others also produced a **wish to be superior**. Comparing with the past gave a sense of progress and insight.
- **Verbatim (abstract):** "only temporal comparisons did so without triggering superiority goals"
- **Follow-up:** Gürel, Brummelman & Overbeek (2022, *Emotion, 22*(6), 1224–1238, https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000920; PubMed). A daily diary with **389** adolescents aged 11–15. On days they compared favourably with their past selves they reported wanting to improve rather than to be superior, and feeling closer to others. Correlational.
- **Limits:** children and teenagers, not adults. A single guided comparison and feelings measured right afterwards. The experiment compares *favourable* comparisons; it does not test what happens when you tell someone who feels behind to "just look at your own progress". Full texts not opened.

**Zell & Alicke (2009) and Zell & Strickhouser (2020) — the counterweight**
- **Citations:**
  - Zell, E., & Alicke, M. D. (2009). Self-evaluative effects of temporal and social comparison. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 45*(1), 223–227. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2008.09.007
  - Zell, E., & Strickhouser, J. E. (2020). Comparisons across dimensions, people, and time: On the primacy of social comparison in self-evaluations. *Social Psychological and Personality Science, 11*(6), 791–800. https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550619884564
- **Findings (abstracts only):**
  - 2009: when people were told both where they ranked against others and whether they were improving or getting worse, **each piece of information independently** shaped how they rated themselves. Outside observers ignored the improvement information and judged only by rank.
  - 2020 (Study 1 N = 393, Study 2 N = 281): feedback about **other people moved self-evaluations and feelings significantly more** than feedback about one's own past.
- **Verbatim (2020 abstract):** "social comparison had a significantly larger effect on self-evaluations and affective reactions than either dimensional or temporal comparison"

**What can honestly be said**
- **CONFIRMED:** people already compare themselves with their past selves a great deal, and it tends to feel good.
- **CONFIRMED (children and teens):** feeling "better than I was" brings pride **without** the urge to be above other people.
- **CONFIRMED:** both kinds of comparison shape how we see ourselves, and in head-to-head tests the comparison with **other people is the stronger one**. You cannot simply switch it off.
- **NOT established:** that advising adults to "compare with your past self instead" improves well-being over weeks or months. I found no such trial. A 2021 review (Morina, *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 16*(6), 1281–1299, https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691620966788) says research on temporal comparison "remains scarce" (read from the open-access copy).
- **Safe wording:** "There's a second ruler you can use: yourself, a year ago. In a study of nearly six hundred kids and teens, both rulers could produce pride. Only one of them also produced the urge to be above everyone else. We can't promise it makes the other comparisons go away. The evidence there is thin."
- **Label as ours:** any "try this" step (for example, "write down one thing you can do now that you couldn't a year ago") is **our suggestion**, not a finding.
- **Common misreports:**
  - "Science says compare yourself only to who you were yesterday." That is a popular saying, not a study result.
  - "Temporal comparison is healthier" as a proven fact for adults.

## B8. Optional: social media (one line only)

- **Verduyn, P., Ybarra, O., Résibois, M., Jonides, J., & Kross, E. (2017).** Do social network sites enhance or undermine subjective well-being? A critical review. *Social Issues and Policy Review, 11*(1), 274–302. https://doi.org/10.1111/sipr.12033
  - **Verbatim (abstract):** "passively using social network sites provokes social comparisons and envy, which have negative downstream consequences for subjective well-being."
  - The review also finds *active* use (posting, messaging) tends to go with better well-being, and says the passive-use finding is the more robust of the two.
- **Verduyn, P., Gugushvili, N., Massar, K., Täht, K., & Kross, E. (2020).** Social comparison on social networking sites. *Current Opinion in Psychology, 36*, 32–37. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2020.04.002 (abstract from PubMed): comparison on these sites "typically" has negative effects because people make upward comparisons, and people already prone to comparing are hit hardest.
- **Vogel, E. A., Rose, J. P., Roberts, L. R., & Eckles, K. (2014).** Social comparison, social media, and self-esteem. *Psychology of Popular Media Culture, 3*(4), 206–222. https://doi.org/10.1037/ppm0000047. Citation confirmed, no retraction. - **What is not established by these sources:** how big the effect is, or that it causes lasting harm for most users. These are reviews of mostly correlational and short-term studies, and the field is contested.
- **Safe wording:** "Reviews of the research suggest that scrolling passively through other people's best moments is the kind of use most tied to comparison and envy."
- **Recommendation:** one line at most, or leave it out. It is not needed for the Vegeta story.

## How the science lines up with the story

Three lessons. Each has one core truth, the beat that mirrors it, the support, and what not to claim.

### Lesson 1 — Why you compare, and with whom

- **Core truth:** *You don't compare yourself with everyone, only with the person close enough to feel like a fair measure of you.*
- **Anime beat:** the scouter that turns a person into a number (Ep 28), and the "elite versus low-class" exchange (Ep 30). Vegeta does not obsess over Frieza, who is far above him, or over Krillin, who is far below. He fixes on the one other Saiyan: same blood, same trade, met again and again, never far apart in strength.
- **Support:** Festinger 1954 (B1: we measure ourselves against similar others, and for abilities we look upward). Kilduff et al. 2010 (B2: rivalry grows from similarity, repeated contests and being evenly matched). Gerber et al. 2018 (B6: given the choice, people look up, and it usually stings).
- **Honest caveats:**
  - Festinger is a theory. The basketball study is about teams. Nobody studied alien princes.
  - The fit is good on "similar, repeated, evenly matched". It is a **stretch** on status: Vegeta is the high-born one resenting someone below him, and the basketball data mostly show lower-status teams fixing on higher-status ones. Do not claim the study explains his pride.
  - Do not say comparison is a flaw. The claim is that it is how minds take a measurement.

### Lesson 2 — The same rival can push you or poison you

- **Core truth:** *A rival makes you better while you want to climb, and worse the moment you'd rather see them fall.*
- **Anime beat, the push:** Vegeta trains alone at 450 times gravity because of Goku and becomes a Super Saiyan (Ep 129).
- **Anime beat, the poison:** the same wish, soured. He lets Babidi's spell in, kills bystanders to force the fight, and tells Goku he had to throw away his family to get his edge back (Eps 228–231). His own words in the dub put his whole **honour** at stake in the result, which is close to the mechanism the 2016 paper measured (self-worth riding on the outcome).
- **Support:**
  - Push: Kilduff 2014 (B3: runners faster with a rival present; VERIFY the digits). Lockwood & Kunda 1997 (B6: someone better inspires when catching up feels possible).
  - Poison: Kilduff et al. 2016 (B4: more fouls and more lying against rivals).
  - The fork: van de Ven et al. 2009 and 2011 (B5: "move up" envy versus "pull down" envy). Chai et al. 2026 (B4: friendly rivalries motivate without the urge to cheat; caring about the contest rather than only the win is what separates them).
- **Honest caveats:**
  - The running and football results are correlations. The envy experiments are small. The 2026 paper is new and measures intentions.
  - **Scale mismatch, say it once:** the studies are about yellow cards and lying in a game for a few dollars. Majin Vegeta kills people. The research mirrors the **direction**, not the size.
  - "Attainable versus unattainable" (Lockwood & Kunda) maps loosely onto Vegeta's swings between training harder and despair. That is our reading, and a **stretch** if stated as fact.
  - Do not say "benign envy is good". Later work shows it has a manipulative side too.

### Lesson 3 — What changes in Ep 280

- **Core truth:** *The comparison stops hurting when "they're better than me" no longer means "I'm worth less", and when the limit you're trying to beat is your own.*
- **Anime beat:** the monologue (Ep 280). Vegeta runs the comparison one last time, loses it, and says so. What he finally sees is that Goku was never fighting to beat *him*: in the dub, Goku fights to test his own limits; in the original, to keep pushing past them. Then (Ep 281) Vegeta steps in for one minute he knows he may not survive, as himself.
- **Support:**
  - Gürel et al. 2020 (B7: measuring against your own past brings pride without the need to be above others; children and teens).
  - Wilson & Ross 2000 (B7: people already use their past selves as a yardstick, and it feels good).
  - Chai et al. 2026 (B4: valuing the process of competing over the outcome).
  - Kilduff et al. 2016 (B4), in reverse: the harm ran through self-worth being tied to the result.
- **Honest caveats:**
  - **This is the weakest-supported lesson, and the script should not hide it.** No study shows that an adult who switches to past-self comparison becomes happier over time, and Zell & Strickhouser 2020 found that comparison with other people still hits harder.
  - Vegeta does **not** stop comparing in this scene. He compares, and accepts the answer. So the lesson is not "stop comparing". It is "change what the comparison is for".
  - "Goku compares himself with his past self" is **our reading** of Vegeta's line, not something the show states in those words. Keep it as "he was racing his own limits".
  - Nobody has studied "admitting someone is better" as an intervention. The admission is the story's beat, not a finding.
  - Any practical step we offer is **our suggestion** and must be labelled so.

**Limits to say out loud at least once:** these studies used basketball teams, amateur runners, football matches, and students doing short tasks, mostly in the US and the Netherlands. The research can say that rivalry and envy come in a form that lifts and a form that corrodes. It cannot promise that a change of yardstick will stop the comparing.

**A closing line the evidence can carry (suggestion, ours):** "You will probably never stop comparing. You can choose what the comparison is for."
