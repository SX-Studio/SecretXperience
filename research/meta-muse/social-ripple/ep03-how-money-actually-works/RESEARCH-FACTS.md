## 1. Bank of England, "Money creation in the modern economy"

Citation: McLeay, M., Radia, A. & Thomas, R. (2014). "Money creation in the modern economy." *Bank of England Quarterly Bulletin* 2014 Q1, pp. 14–27.
Source: https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/2014/money-creation-in-the-modern-economy.pdf (PDF text extracted and read directly)

### 1a. Loans create deposits: VERIFIED
- Quote (Overview, p. 14): "the principal way is through commercial banks making loans. Whenever a bank makes a loan, it simultaneously creates a matching deposit in the borrower's bank account, thereby creating new money."
- Also: "Rather than banks receiving deposits when households save and then lending them out, bank lending creates deposits."
- Also (p. 15): "those bank deposits are mostly created by commercial banks themselves."
- **Safe narration:** "According to the Bank of England, when a bank makes a loan it simultaneously creates a matching deposit, which is new money."

### 1b. Repaying loans destroys money: VERIFIED
- Quote (p. 16): "Just as taking out a new loan creates money, the repayment of bank loans destroys money."
- Also (p. 17): "Banks making loans and consumers repaying them are the most significant ways in which bank deposits are created and destroyed in the modern economy. But they are far from the only ways." (Buying and selling assets, and issuing long-term debt or equity, also create or destroy deposits.)
- **Safe narration:** "And when that loan is paid back, the money it created disappears again."

### 1c. The "97%" figure: VERIFIED, but needs a date and a scope
- Yes, the figure is in this paper. Quote (p. 15): "Of the two types of broad money, bank deposits make up the vast majority — 97% of the amount currently in circulation." Footnote (6): "As of December 2013."
- Scope: **UK** broad money (the BoE's M4ex measure). The rest (~3%) is currency, meaning notes and coins. The figure is a snapshot from December 2013, not a constant.
- Do not say "97% of all money in the world". Also, "97% is deposits" is not the same as "banks create 97% of money". The paper only says deposits are "mostly" created by commercial banks.
- **Safe narration:** "In the UK, bank deposits made up about 97% of broad money at the end of 2013; the rest was notes and coins."

### 1d. Limits on lending: the money multiplier vs profitability, regulation and Bank Rate: VERIFIED
- Against the multiplier (p. 14): "In normal times, the central bank does not fix the amount of money in circulation, nor is central bank money 'multiplied up' into more loans and deposits."
- The limits (p. 14, the best quotable passage): "Although commercial banks create money through lending, they cannot do so freely without limit. Banks are limited in how much they can lend if they are to remain profitable in a competitive banking system. Prudential regulation also acts as a constraint on banks' activities in order to maintain the resilience of the financial system."
- Ultimate limit (p. 14): "Monetary policy acts as the ultimate limit on money creation."
- Bank Rate (p. 18): interest rates on banks' assets and liabilities "depend on the policy rate set by the Bank of England, which acts as the ultimate constraint on money creation."
- Profit (p. 18): "Competition for loans and deposits, and the desire to make a profit, therefore limit money creation by banks."
- Multiplier reversed (p. 20): "So, in reality, the theory of the money multiplier operates in the reverse way to that normally described."
- Third limit the script should not leave out: the behaviour of households and companies. They "could quickly 'destroy' money by using it to repay their existing debt".
- Note: this is the Bank of England's description of the UK system in normal times. It is now widely accepted by central banks, but some textbook treatments still teach the multiplier.
- **Safe narration:** "Banks can't create money without limit: the Bank of England says they're held back by whether lending is profitable, by regulation, and ultimately by the interest rate the central bank sets, not by a fixed 'money multiplier'."

## 2. Ray Dalio, "How The Economic Machine Works" (2013 animated video)

Source: video by Ray Dalio / Bridgewater, published on YouTube 22 Sept 2013 (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PHe0bXAIuk0). Hub: https://www.bridgewater.com/research-library/how-the-economic-machine-works (also economicprinciples.org).
The transcript was checked at https://singjupost.com/economy-really-work-ray-dalio-transcript/. That is a third-party transcript, so check timestamps against the video before quoting on screen.

### 2a. "One person's spending is another person's income": VERIFIED
- Exact lines in the transcript: "This is because one person's spending is another person's income." Also: "Think about it, every dollar you spend, someone else earns." And in the worked example: "Since your spending is another person's income, someone is earning $110,000."
- **Safe narration:** "As Ray Dalio puts it, one person's spending is another person's income."

### 2b. Cycle lengths: VERIFIED as Dalio's framework (not a law)
- Quote: "Debt swings occur in two big cycles. One takes about 5 to 8 years and the other takes about 75 to 100 years."
- Also: "The short term debt cycle typically lasts 5 to 8 years and happens over and over again for decades."
- Caveat: these are Dalio's own rough estimates from his model of the economy. They are not a consensus result in academic macroeconomics. Attribute them to him.
- **Safe narration:** "In Dalio's model, short-term debt cycles last roughly 5 to 8 years and long-term ones roughly 75 to 100 years."

### 2c. Credit pulls spending forward, so spending must fall later: VERIFIED
- Quotes: "In order to buy something you can't afford, you need to spend more than you make." / "In doing so you create a time in the future that you need to spend less than you make in order to pay it back." / "Think of borrowing as simply a way of pulling spending forward."
- **Safe narration:** "Borrowing lets you spend more than you earn today, but it means you'll have to spend less than you earn later to pay it back."

## 3. Yap rai stone that sank at sea: VERIFIED as a reported story (hearsay, keep the caveat)

Primary source: William Henry Furness III, *The Island of Stone Money: Uap of the Carolines* (Philadelphia: J.B. Lippincott, 1910). Full text: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/72830 (read directly; the passage is in the chapter on fei, "Sunken wealth", p. 97 per the index).

- Quote: Furness's informant Fatumak told him of "a family whose wealth was unquestioned,--acknowledged by every one, and yet no one, not even the family itself, had ever laid eye or hand on this wealth; it consisted of an enormous _fei_ ... at that very time it was lying at the bottom of the sea!"
- Quote: "A violent storm arose and the party, to save their lives, were obliged to cut the raft adrift, and the stone sank out of sight."
- Quote: "The purchasing power of that stone remains, therefore, as valid as if it were leaning visibly against the side of the owner's house".
- Details to keep accurate:
  - The stone sank while being **towed on a raft** in a storm, coming back from a quarrying expedition (the stones were quarried in Palau).
  - It sank "two or three generations" before Furness's visit.
  - The owners' wealth was accepted because the crew all "testified" to its size and quality and that it "was lost through no fault of the owner".
  - The story is **second-hand**: Furness heard it from Fatumak and did not see it happen.
- Milton Friedman, "The Island of Stone Money", Hoover Institution Working Papers in Economics, February 1991; reprinted as chapter 1 of *Money Mischief* (1992). Friedman retells Furness's account and compares it with gold sitting in vaults. **Not directly verified:** the Hoover PDF could not be fetched (403). Bibliographic details are confirmed via the Habele Institute catalogue, and NPR Planet Money (https://www.npr.org/sections/money/2011/02/15/131934618/The-island-of-stone-money) confirms that Friedman's paper uses the sunken-stone story.
- **Safe narration:** "An American visitor to Yap in the early 1900s was told of a family considered rich because of a giant stone that had sunk in a storm generations earlier; nobody had seen it, but everyone agreed it was theirs."

## 4. Troy, Shallcross & Mauss (2013): VERIFIED (single correlational study)

Citation: Troy, A. S., Shallcross, A. J., & Mauss, I. B. (2013). A person-by-situation approach to emotion regulation: Cognitive reappraisal can either help or hurt, depending on the context. *Psychological Science*, 24(12), 2505–2514. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613496434 (PubMed PMID 24145331; abstract checked)

- **Sample size: 170 participants.** Quote: "we measured cognitive-reappraisal ability, the severity of recent life stressors, stressor controllability, and level of depression in 170 participants."
- **Finding:** "For participants with uncontrollable stress, higher cognitive-reappraisal ability was associated with lower levels of depression. In contrast, for participants with controllable stress, higher cognitive-reappraisal ability was associated with greater levels of depression."
- Caveats: this is a single cross-sectional, correlational study, so it shows association, not cause. The "help" and "hurt" findings depend on an interaction effect. Use "linked with", never "causes".
- **Safe narration:** "One 2013 study of 170 people found that being good at reframing stress was linked to less depression when the stress was out of their control, but more depression when they could have changed the situation."

## 5. Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir & Zhao (2013), "Poverty impedes cognitive function": DISPUTED

Citation: *Science* 341(6149): 976–980. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1238041 (PubMed abstract checked; full text paywalled)

### 5a. Mall study, $1,500 vs $150 car repair: VERIFIED (scenario wording via the replication materials)
- Shoppers at a New Jersey mall ("roughly 400 subjects", per Princeton's press release on ScienceDaily: https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/08/130829145125.htm). They were asked to think through hypothetical financial problems, "such as $150 or $1,500 for the car repair", and then did Raven's matrices and a cognitive-control task.
- The scenario wording is confirmed by the replication write-up on OSF (https://osf.io/j6dpm/): "Your car is having some trouble and requires $1500 [$150] to be fixed."
- Original result: poorer participants did worse in the "hard" ($1,500) condition. Richer participants were unaffected.

### 5b. Indian sugarcane farmers: VERIFIED
- 464 sugarcane farmers in 54 villages around Villupuram and Tiruvannamalai, Tamil Nadu (Oxford Mind & Behaviour Research Group: https://mbrg.bsg.ox.ac.uk/mind-and-behaviour-projects/poverty-impedes-cognitive-function).
- Abstract: "the same farmer shows diminished cognitive performance before harvest, when poor, as compared with after harvest, when rich."
- Press release: the farmers rely on the harvest "for at least 60 percent of their income".

### 5c. The "13 IQ points" comparison: VERIFIED as the authors' framing (it is a conversion, not a measured IQ)
- Princeton press release: the effect was "a drop in cognitive function similar to a 13-point dip in IQ, or the loss of an entire night's sleep." University of Warwick news (https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/news/2013/9/poverty): "reduced the results on IQ tests by an average of 13 points."
- The 13 points is the authors' conversion of the mall-study Raven's effect. Nobody's IQ was actually measured as dropping. Given 5d, **do not put "13 IQ points" on screen as an established fact.**

### 5d. Replication debate
- **Wicherts & Zand Scholten (2013), Comment, *Science* 342(6163): 1169**, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1246680. "A reanalysis without dichotomization of income fails to corroborate their findings". They attribute the interactions to ceiling effects on short, easy tests.
  - **Authors' reply (Mani et al. 2013, *Science* 342: 1169)**, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1246799: "using a continuous income variable, the interaction between income, and experimental manipulation remains reliable across our experiments", and the post-harvest improvement "is robust to the presence of learning."
- **Carvalho, Meier & Wang (2016), *American Economic Review* 106(2): 260–84**, https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20140481. Low-income US households were randomly assigned to take a survey before or after payday. Quote: their findings "do not support the hypothesis that financial strain per se impedes cognitive function and worsens the quality of decision-making. We find that participants surveyed before and after payday performed similarly on a number of cognitive function tasks." (PDF: https://eml.berkeley.edu/~cle/alluc/Carvalho_Meier_Wang.pdf)
  - **Reply:** Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir & Zhao (2020), "Scarcity and Cognitive Function around Payday: A Conceptual and Empirical Analysis," *J. Assoc. Consumer Research* 5(4): 365–376, https://doi.org/10.1086/709885. They reanalyse the Carvalho et al. data by exact days to payday and argue for a "scarcity-induced cognitive load, closer to payday". This is the original authors' rebuttal.
- **O'Donnell et al. (2021), *PNAS* 118(44): e2103313118**, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2103313118 (full text via PMC8612349). This is a preregistered audit of 20 scarcity studies, each run at 2.5× the original sample.
  - Quote: "Of the 20 studies that were significant in the original, four of our replication efforts yielded significant results." And: "scarcity primes have a minimal influence on cognitive ability, product attitudes, or well being."
  - It included **Mani et al. Study 4** (the car-repair / Raven's design; OSF write-up by Antonoplis, https://osf.io/j6dpm/), with N = 229 valid MTurk participants. Quote: "Contrary to prior results (Mani et al., 2013), there was no robust interaction between income and condition (F(1,224) = 0.17, p = .678)."
  - Caveats: this was online (MTurk), not in a mall, and only one of the original lab studies was retested. The PNAS authors themselves note that field studies with hard-to-reach populations are harder to replicate.
- I found no large multi-site, many-labs-style replication of the mall study, and no direct replication of the farmer field study.

### Current status (2 sentences)
The 2013 lab finding that thinking about a big hypothetical bill lowers poorer people's test scores has not held up well: a preregistered, larger online replication of one of the mall experiments found no effect, and a large US payday study found no before/after difference in cognitive performance, though the original authors dispute that interpretation. The broader idea that money worries can tax attention is still debated rather than settled, so the "13 IQ points" figure should be presented as a contested early claim, not an established fact.

- **Safe narration:** "A famous 2013 study suggested money worries can tax our thinking, but later attempts to replicate it have produced mixed results, so scientists still disagree on how big that effect really is."

## 6. Paradox of thrift (Keynes): VERIFIED

Source: Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, *Page One Economics*, E. Katarina Vermann, "Wait, Is Saving Good or Bad? The Paradox of Thrift," May 2012. https://www.stlouisfed.org/publications/page-one-economics/2012/05/01/wait-is-saving-good-or-bad-the-paradox-of-thrift
- Quote (glossary): the paradox of thrift is "A controversial Keynesian economics theory, which proposes that if everyone tries to save more during a recession, then aggregate demand will fall." "As a result, the theory argues everyone would grow poorer instead of richer."
- Note: the Fed itself calls the theory "controversial". It applies mainly to a slump with spare capacity. Over the long run, higher saving can fund investment and growth. Keep the "can" and the "at once / in a downturn" qualifiers.
- I found no IMF educational page on this in my searches. The St. Louis Fed is authoritative enough.
- **Safe narration:** "Keynes pointed out a paradox: if everyone tries to save more at the same time, especially in a downturn, total spending falls, and total income can fall with it."

## 7. Double coincidence of wants, and the "barter came first" myth

### 7a. Double coincidence of wants: VERIFIED as the classic textbook argument
- W. Stanley Jevons, *Money and the Mechanism of Exchange* (1875), Ch. I "Barter": "to allow of an act of barter, there must be a double coincidence, which will rarely happen." https://www.econlib.org/library/YPDBooks/Jevons/jvnMME.html?chapter_num=2

### 7b. Pure barter economies preceding money: NOT supported by anthropology. Do NOT narrate "first there was barter" as history.
- Caroline Humphrey (1985), "Barter and Economic Disintegration," *Man* (New Series) 20(1): 48–72. Quote (PDF read directly): "No example of a barter economy, pure and simple, has ever been described, let alone the emergence from it of money; all available ethnography suggests that there never has been such a thing."
- Also: "The mainstream economists' view that barter should be seen as a 'natural' phenomenon of human nature and as the origin of money is rejected. Barter occurs in specific socio-economic conditions which may obtain also in economies which know money."
- David Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011), ch. 2 "The Myth of Barter", builds on Humphrey. He argues that the barter documented in the record mostly happens between people already familiar with money (for example when currency collapses), and that credit and debt arrangements came before coins. I did not verify a direct Graeber quote (the hosting pages blocked fetch), so paraphrase him and do not quote him.
- Counterpoint for balance: some economists (for example George Selgin, Cato, 2016) argue that the absence of documented barter economies does not disprove the logic of the coincidence-of-wants argument. That is fair: the *logic* holds, the *origin story* is not documented.
- **Safe narration:** "Economists use barter to show why money is handy: a direct swap needs a 'double coincidence of wants', where I have what you want and you have what I want. But anthropologists have never found a society that ran on pure barter before money, so think of this as a thought experiment, not history."
