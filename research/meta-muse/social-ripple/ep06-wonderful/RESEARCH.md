## Notes per study

### 1. Koo, Algoe, Wilson & Gilbert (2008), JPSP 95(5):1217–1224
- **Study 1:** 65 University of Virginia students (60 analysed). Each picked a positive event they felt grateful for, from categories such as education, health, an act of kindness, or an achievement. They were then randomised to one of four conditions:
  - **Absence:** "describe ways that this thing or event might never have happened…", "ways in which it is SURPRISING…"
  - **Presence:** "happened easily or was certain to become part of your life…", "NOT AT ALL SURPRISING…"
  - **Grateful control:** just describe the event.
  - **No writing.**
- **Measure:** a 13-item affect index on 7-point scales.
- **Study 1 result:** the absence condition was higher than the other three combined, F(1,56) = 5.78, p = .02. The other three did not differ.
- **Study 2:** N = 120, replicated (p = .002). Surprise was a tentative mediator, only marginal.
- **Study 3:** 46 forecasters (42 analysed) predicted their affect for each condition. They did not predict the absence advantage and leaned the opposite way, though not significantly (p = .15). A role × condition interaction across studies was significant (p < .01).
- **Study 4:** people in exclusive relationships of 5+ years (mean 13.7). They wrote for 15–20 min either how they met their partner or how they might never have met. Satisfaction change was largest in the absence condition (p = .049). Of 16 forecasters, 14 preferred the "how we met" task, and they predicted feeling worse after the absence task (p = .06).
- **Authors' own caveat:** effects were modest, and samples were already happy, which restricted range.

### 2. Fowler & Christakis (2010), PNAS 107(12):5334–5338
- **Data:** secondary analysis of existing public-goods experiments, which the paper attributes to Fehr & Gächter (2002). 240 subjects in groups of four, reshuffled every period so that no two subjects met twice, for 6 periods per game.
- **Game rules:** each subject had a 20 MU endowment, and each MU contributed returned 0.4 MU to every group member. There was a basic version and a version with costly punishment.
- **Findings:** a focal person's later contribution rose with what earlier group-mates had contributed, and this carried to people who were never in the original group.
  - Basic game: +0.19 MU per 1 MU at one degree; +0.07 at two degrees (p = .0005).
  - Punishment game: +0.18 at one degree; +0.05 at two degrees; +0.06 at three degrees (95% CI 0.02–0.09).
  - So "up to three degrees" rests on the punishment version.
- **Authors' limits:** relationships were anonymous and not sustained; the result applies to this experimentally controlled network; real-world spread "remains an open question".
- **For S04:** the Theo → Bea → neighbour chain is our **story** illustration. The study is about money contributions among anonymous lab players, not friends passing along favours. On screen, label it "in a lab game".

### 3. Kumar & Epley (2023), JEP: General 152(1):236–252
- **Version note:** published online Aug 2022, in the Jan 2023 print issue, so "2022" vs "2023" both appear. Numbers were read from the authors' in-press manuscript (OSF preprint 10.31234/osf.io/3yast) and cross-checked against a later in-press copy (hot chocolate and dictator-game means match).
- **Exp 1a:** 106 MBA students did any kind act they chose (coffee, rides, flowers, baking). Recipients rated the act "bigger" and felt more positive than performers expected.
- **Exp 1b:** 100 participants mailed a kind card. The mood gap was small (d = 0.28) and there was no awkwardness gap.
- **Exp 2a (hot chocolate):** 84 people were recruited over two winter weekends at a park skating ribbon. Each could keep a hot chocolate or give it to a stranger; 9 kept it, leaving 75 pairs.
  - "How big" the act was (0–10): givers 3.76 vs recipients 7.00.
  - Recipient mood (−5 to +5): givers expected 2.73, actual 3.52 (p = .0001, d = 0.48).
  - The experimenter told recipients that the giver had chosen to give it away.
- **Exp 2b:** 51 lab pairs, gift from a "lab store". Mood expected 2.08 vs actual 3.47.
- **Exp 3 (cupcake, summer, park):**
  - Kindness condition: bigness 6.35 vs 7.41; mood 2.96 vs 3.51 (p = .056, marginal).
  - Control (cupcake given by the experimenter, not as a kindness): no miscalibration. The extra value comes from the gesture being kind.
  - Performers themselves felt better than usual (M = 2.90).
- **Exp 4:** dictator game, described in K2 above.
- **Exps 5a–5b:** lower expected impact is linked to lower willingness to do the act.
- **Mechanism:** recipients respond to the **warmth** of the act; givers judge the object itself.

### 4. Zhao & Epley (2021), JPSP 121(2):239–256 (not *Psych Science*)
- **Scope:** a series of surveys plus nine experiments: main Exps 1–4 plus supplemental experiments, of which the abstract names S2, S3 and S4.
- **Exp 1:** 210 people recruited in pairs at a public park in "an ethnically diverse neighborhood in a large U.S. city". There were 52 pairs per condition. Pairs were friends, family and couples, who had known each other about 10.8 years on average.
  - Expressers wrote 3 compliments they had "noticed but have not… had a chance to" say.
  - Positive-mood composite (0–10): expressers predicted 7.79 vs recipients' 8.81 (p < .001).
  - Awkwardness (0–10): predicted 3.46 vs actual 2.06 (p = .004).
  - In the control condition (no compliments), predictors were accurate (7.50 vs 7.81), so this is not a general tendency to underestimate others' mood.
  - Expressers underestimated how **competent** their wording would seem (6.62 vs 8.59) more than how warm (8.12 vs 9.16).
- **Surveys:** people say they give compliments less often than they think they should. In one survey (N = 103), they reported withholding compliments to close others about 36.4% of the time, against 62.2% for criticisms. This is self-report.
- **Exp 4:** focusing on the warmth of a compliment increased willingness to give it.

### 5. Kumar & Epley (2018), Psychological Science 29(9):1423–1435
- **Exp 1:** 107 MBA students wrote a gratitude letter to "someone who had touched their life in a meaningful way". Most were encouraged to write to another MBA student. 80 of 92 contactable recipients responded.
  - Expressers underestimated recipients' surprise at receiving the letter (d = 0.68), surprise at its content (d = 0.40), and positive mood (d = 0.74). They overestimated awkwardness (d = 0.32).
  - Means as reported in the paper, expressers vs recipients:
    - Surprise at receiving (0–10): 6.01 vs 7.88
    - Surprise at content (0–10): 4.45 vs 5.66
    - Mood (−5 to +5): 3.11 vs 4.12
    - Awkward (0–10): 2.95 vs 1.95
  - Expressers' own mood: 2.58 after writing vs 0.46 at baseline.
- **Exp 2:** 100 lab participants could write to anyone (parents, friends, teachers), with 58 recipients responding. The mood, content-surprise and awkwardness results replicated. Surprise at receiving did **not** replicate.
- **Exps 3a–3b:** higher expected positivity and lower expected awkwardness went with greater willingness to send a letter.
- **Mechanism:** expressers worry about getting the words "just right" (competence); recipients feel the warmth.
- **Experiment count:** the abstract says "three experiments", but the body reports Exps 1, 2, 3a, 3b and a final competence/warmth experiment. Do not put an experiment count on screen.
- **Fit for S07:** Theo writing to Mo matches the design. Theo is the expresser, so the finding is about *Theo's* fear that the note would be awkward, not Mo's.

## Shaky or commonly misquoted claims in this space

1. **"Kindness / happiness is contagious up to three degrees."**
   - The PNAS 2010 paper is a **lab money game among anonymous strangers**, and the three-degree step appears only in the punishment version.
   - The broader "three degrees of influence" idea comes from Fowler & Christakis's **observational** Framingham analyses, e.g. *BMJ* 2008; 337:a2338, doi:10.1136/bmj.a2338: "happiness… extends up to three degrees of separation".
   - Those analyses were challenged on confounding. Cohen-Cole & Fletcher (2008), *BMJ* 337:a2533, doi:10.1136/bmj.a2533, found similar "network effects" for acne, headaches and **height** using standard methods, and the effects vanished once shared environment was controlled.
   - The authors' own review calls their methods subject to "known and often unavoidable limitations" (Christakis & Fowler 2013, *Stat Med* 32(4):556–577, doi:10.1002/sim.5408).
   - **Safe:** "in a lab game". **Not safe:** "science proves your kindness reaches the friends of your friends' friends".
2. **"Every good deed is tripled."** This misreads F2. It describes money units in a six-round anonymous lab game, and the authors limit it to that network structure.
3. **"Imagining your life without something makes you happier" as a lasting fix.** Koo et al. tested a single writing session with an immediate mood measure. The effects were modest, and the samples were already happy.
4. **"Recipients are X times happier than you think."** No such multiplier appears in Kumar & Epley (2018, 2023) or Zhao & Epley (2021). The gaps are on rating scales, moderate in size (d of about 0.3–0.75 for mood), and expressers already expected positive reactions.
5. **"Zhao & Epley (2021), Psychological Science."** Wrong journal: it is *JPSP* 121(2). The Zhao & Epley paper in *Psychological Science* is the 2022 help-seeking paper ("Surprisingly happy to have helped", 33(10):1708–1731, doi:10.1177/09567976221097615).
6. **Kumar & Epley kindness paper "2022 vs 2023".** Both are correct. Cite it as 2023, *JEP: General* 152(1):236–252 (published online 2022).
7. **"Over 5,900 participants" (Liu et al.).** Press-release figure only, UNVERIFIED. **"Hot chocolate in Chicago's Maggie Daley Park"** is not stated in the paper, which says only "a major U.S. city": UNVERIFIED.
8. **Kindness-chemistry claims seen in the same posts** (e.g. "a kind act boosts serotonin in giver, receiver and onlooker"). No primary source was checked or found in this pass. Treat them as UNVERIFIED and do not use them.

- **S03:** use K1. Mo's free sandwich to Theo is the story; the hot-chocolate study is the evidence.
- **S04:** use F1, with "in a lab game" on screen. K2 (kindness recipients gave more to a stranger) is a gentler, more literal match for "a kindness passed on" and avoids the three-degree overreach.
