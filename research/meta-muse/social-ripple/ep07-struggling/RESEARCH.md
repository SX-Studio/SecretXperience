# Ep7 research: facts for "What If You Could See How Much Everyone Was Secretly Struggling?" (verified 2026-11-05)

How each claim was checked:
- **Full text** was read for:
  - Jordan et al. 2011 (PMC4138214)
  - Flynn & Lake 2008 (APA-permitted author version, Cornell DigitalCommons@ILR)
  - Bohns 2016 (author manuscript, Cornell eCommons)
  - Huang et al. 2017 (publisher PDF)
- **Abstract or official record only** for:
  - Chou & Edge 2012, Prentice & Miller 1993, Liu et al. 2023, Hunt et al. 2018, Allcott et al. 2020 and Orben & Przybylski 2019 (PubMed or Crossref)
  - the Huang et al. 2025 correction notice (PubMed)
  - the WHO and NIMH pages
- **UNVERIFIED** marks anything that came only from secondary write-ups, or that I did not check at all.

## Corrections to the brief
- **Flynn & Lake's title is "If you need *help*, just ask"**, not "If you need support, just ask".
- **Chou & Edge is dated 2012** (*CBSN* 15(2), print issue). It was published online on 14 Dec 2011, so "2011" also appears in some citations. Cite it as 2012.
- **WHO's headline figure is now "Nearly 1 in 7 people in the world live with a mental disorder"** (2023 data; fact sheet dated 11 Sep 2026). The widely quoted "1 in 8" (2019 data) is **out of date**.
- **The Huang et al. 2017 paper has a 2025 correction** (doi:10.1037/pspi0000491). An independent audit found "several minor errors in how some results were reported" and "confirmed that all the conclusions in the paper are valid". It also "found no reason to doubt the integrity of the data collection". Francesca Gino is a co-author. See note 6.

## Script mismatches to fix in BEATS.md
1. **S03, "and that makes us lonelier":** this is **causal and not supported**. Jordan Study 3 is a correlation in 104 students, and the authors say the direction could run either way. Use "is linked to feeling lonelier".
2. **S06 uses Flynn & Lake for the wrong action.** Flynn & Lake measured how often strangers say yes when *you ask them for help*. In S06, YOU is *checking in on* Ana, not asking for help. There are two fixes:
   - (a) Keep Flynn & Lake but put it on a help-seeking moment: Ana asking for help, or YOU asking someone for help in S07.
   - (b) For the check-in itself, use Liu et al. 2023 (people underestimate how much a check-in is appreciated). Liu was already used in Ep4 (n25) and Ep6 (L1), so either make it a deliberate callback or use (a).
3. **S06/S08, "ask twice":** **no study we verified tests asking "How are you, really?" twice.** Huang et al. 2017 shows that asking more *follow-up questions* is linked to being better liked in get-to-know-you chats between strangers. It does not show that re-asking makes people open up. Keep "ask twice" as the narrator's gentle suggestion, not as a study claim. See row Q1.
4. **S04, highlight reels:** the strongest verified support comes from Jordan Study 1A. Negative emotional experiences were more often effortfully hidden than positive ones (40% vs 13%). That supports "everyone shows their sunny side" offline, with no social-media causation claim needed. The social-media studies are optional and need careful wording (rows SM1–SM3).

## Notes per study

### 1. Jordan, Monin, Dweck, Lovett, John & Gross (2011), *PSPB* 37(1):120–135
- **Pilot:** 122 intro-psychology students at a West Coast university and 225 at an East Coast university. They estimated what share of classmates had, in the last month, felt really depressed for a day, felt very lonely on a weekend night, cried from sadness, or felt overwhelmed by work.
  - At one university, estimates vs self-reports were 52% vs 78%, 38% vs 56%, 43% vs 66%, and 78% vs 94%.
  - At the other, they were 54% vs 68%, 42% vs 62%, 41% vs 59%, and 82% vs 92%.
  - **Do not put these on screen as "78% of students were depressed".** "Felt really depressed for a day" is a colloquial self-report item, not clinical depression. It is also a pilot study.
- **Study 1A:** N = 63 first-years. Negative experiences were more covert than positive ones:
  - private (no one present): 29% vs 15%
  - effortfully hidden: 40% vs 13%
  - never shared afterward: 36% vs 26%
- **Study 1B:** N = 80 first-years, with two $50 prizes for accuracy. They estimated peer prevalence over the past two weeks for 6 negative and 6 positive experiences generated in Study 1A.
  - Every negative experience was underestimated (average −17.2 points).
  - For the positive experiences, 3 were overestimated, 1 underestimated, and 2 were accurate (average +5.6 points, not significantly different from zero).
  - Example items: "had a distressing fight or argument with a friend or partner"; "felt sad because they missed people outside the university".
- **Study 2:** an existing dataset with 214 enrolled and 140 complete. Weekly self-ratings over a 10-week term were compared with end-of-term ratings by up to 3 peers. Peers underestimated negative emotions (mean error −0.35 on a 5-point scale) and overestimated positive ones (+0.19). Preferential suppression of negative emotion partly mediated the gap (n = 123).
- **Study 3:** N = 104 paid online students. Event items were stripped of intensity words.
  - Negative experiences were underestimated by an average of 21.4 points; positive ones were overestimated by 3.8 (not significant).
  - Lower negative estimates went with more loneliness (UCLA scale), more brooding, and lower life satisfaction. Higher positive estimates went with lower life satisfaction.
  - There was no link with CES-D depression or subjective happiness, and no link between number of confidants and accuracy.
- **Authors' framing, usable as narration:** "people may think they are more alone in their emotional difficulties than they really are." The paper opens with a happy-hour vignette of three coworkers who all say they're "fine". It is almost exactly our S01, so the scene is a fair dramatisation.
- **Limits:** student samples, small Ns, and cross-sectional well-being data. **I did not check replication status.**

### 2. Flynn & Lake (2008), *JPSP* 95(1):128–143
- **Study 1:** 52 recruited and 42 completed. Each had to get 5 strangers on campus to fill out a 5–10 min questionnaire, using only the script "Will you fill out a questionnaire?". Half predicted beforehand; predicted 20.5 vs actual 10.5, paired t(22) = 3.99. Making a prediction did not change actual compliance.
- **Study 2:** 48 recruited, 13 withdrew, and 35 started.
  - Phone task: borrow a stranger's phone; 3 yeses needed. Predicted 10.1, actual 6.2.
  - Escort task: ask a stranger to walk them to the gym; 1 yes needed, with an experimenter shadowing. Predicted 7.2, actual 2.3.
  - Combined: 8.61 vs 5.12. 79% of those who completed made the error.
- **Study 3:** Team in Training fundraisers (127 recruited, 124 pre-survey, 91 post-survey). Fundraising goals ran from $2,100 to $5,000. Predicted 210.3 vs actual 122.2 people asked. Average donation predicted at $48.33 vs $63.80 actual (24% more).
- **Studies 4–5:** vignettes. People imagining themselves as the helper gave higher compliance estimates than people imagining themselves as the help-seeker (49.6% vs 34.3% in Study 4). Help-seekers neglect how awkward it is to say no.
- **Study 6:** 79 completed. Asking directly reproduced the underestimate (predicted 5.22 vs actual 2.28 asks per yes). **With flyers the error reversed** (predicted 7.58 vs actual 15.32). The size of the request (1 page vs 10 pages) changed predictions but not actual compliance.
- **Abstract wording:** "underestimated by as much as 50% the likelihood that others would agree". The "needed to ask about half as many people as predicted" framing fits Study 1 (20.5 → 10.5). Study 3 is about 58% (122/210), and Study 2 combined is about 59%. **Say "about half" only for Study 1.** Across studies, "far fewer than they expected" is safe.

### 3. Bohns (2016), *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 25(2):119–123
- A short review of Flynn & Lake 2008, Bohns et al. 2011, Bohns, Roghanizad & Xu 2014, Newark, Flynn & Bohns 2014, Roghanizad & Bohns 2015, and others. Table 1 covers 12 studies from 5 articles.
- **Verified abstract claims:** "more than 14,000 strangers", and "on average, requesters underestimate compliance by 48%".
- **Mechanism:** requesters fail to appreciate how awkward it is to say "no".
- **Moderators:**
  - Repeated requests: after an initial refusal, requesters expected a second request's compliance to drop by 16%. It actually rose by 10% (Newark et al. 2014). **This is a different second favour, not re-asking "how are you", so do not map it onto "ask twice".**
  - Money incentives: reduce the error, but targets were "just as willing to comply for free".
  - Culture: the error is smaller among Chinese than US participants.
  - Medium: email and flyers flip the error to overestimation.
- **Limit (stated by the author):** most targets were strangers. Effects with close others are untested in this review.

### 4. Social comparison and social media
- **Chou & Edge (2012):** a survey of 425 undergraduates at a Utah state university. Measures were years on Facebook, hours per week, number of friends, and agreement that others are happier, have better lives, and that life is fair.
  - Longer use went with more agreement that others were happier and less agreement that life is fair.
  - More weekly time went with more agreement that others were happier and had better lives.
  - More Facebook "friends" they didn't know personally went with more agreement that others had better lives.
  - **Weak for our purpose:** cross-sectional, one campus, 2011-era Facebook, and no effect sizes verified.
- **Recommended replacement for S04:** Jordan Study 1A (J4), which needs no social-media causation claim. If the feed visual stays, pair it with a neutral line: "Online, like in person, we mostly share the sunny parts." That is an observation, not a study claim. You may also attach SM1 with "in one survey" wording.
- **Stronger experimental evidence (optional):** Hunt et al. 2018 (SM2), a single small RCT, and Allcott et al. 2020 (SM3), a large deactivation RCT with economic and well-being outcomes. Neither tests "others look happier" directly.

### 5. Prentice & Miller (1993), *JPSP* 64(2):243–256
- See row P1. Use it as conceptual background for "everyone hides it, so everyone thinks they're alone". Jordan et al. explicitly build on this tradition.

### 6. Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino (2017), *JPSP* 113(3):430–452
- **Study 1:** 430 recruited and 398 analysed (199 pairs) in a Harvard behavioural lab. Pairs held 15-min anonymous instant-message chats. One partner was told to ask "at least nine" or "at most four" questions (the 75th and 25th percentiles from a pilot). The uninstructed partner liked high askers more: original paper M = 5.79 vs 5.31, d = .35. Askers did not predict this.
- **Study 2A/2B:** online pairs (338 people, 169 pairs), with both partners manipulated. In 2B, third-party observers did not like high askers more. Follow-up questions specifically drove the effect, through perceived responsiveness ("listening, understanding, validation, and care").
- **Study 3:** 110 speed daters with 1,961 date decisions (correlational). A higher rate of follow-up questions predicted more second-date yeses.
- **Integrity note:** the 2025 correction followed an independent audit. It found minor reporting errors, confirmed all conclusions, and "found no reason to doubt the integrity of the data collection" (PubMed record of the correction). Gino's separate data-fraud case involves other papers. If the channel's policy is to avoid Gino co-authored work entirely, drop Q1. The episode does not need it.
- **Fit:** it supports "ask, then ask a follow-up" as warmth and responsiveness. It does not support "asking twice gets the truth".

## Popular claims in this space: solid vs shaky
1. **"Instagram/social media causes depression."** **Shaky as stated.** Most evidence is correlational, and the average association is small (Orben & Przybylski 2019: at most 0.4% of variance). Experimental evidence is mixed and modest (Hunt et al. 2018; Allcott et al. 2020). The debate is active. Widely quoted lines from Meta's leaked internal slides (WSJ "Facebook Files", 2021) are not peer-reviewed research: UNVERIFIED here, do not quote. **Do not make any causal social-media claim on screen.**
2. **"Duck syndrome"** (calm on the surface, paddling furiously underneath). A colloquial campus term, not a recognised diagnosis or research construct. Use it as a metaphor only, without "science says". The duck visual is fine; "Duck syndrome is real" is not. (No primary research source exists to verify.)
3. **"Smiling depression."** A popular term, not a formal diagnosis. Avoid it: it's diagnostic language and off-tone.
4. **"Everyone you meet is fighting a battle you know nothing about."** Often attributed to Plato or others; the attribution is UNVERIFIED and likely spurious. If used, leave it unattributed, or better, don't use it.
5. **Prevalence statistics** ("1 in 4", "1 in 5", "1 in 8", "most people are struggling"). Only official wording is acceptable (row X1). "Most people are secretly struggling" has **no source**; do not say it. Jordan et al. show we *underestimate* how common negative experiences are, which is different from "most people are struggling".
6. **"People are 48% more willing to help than you think."** Misquote risk. The 48% is the average underestimate of compliance across one lab's studies of requests to strangers (Bohns 2016), and it includes requests people found uncomfortable or unethical.
7. **"Ask twice"** as a researched technique. No study verified here. It is associated with a UK mental-health campaign: UNVERIFIED, not checked. Present it as the narrator's suggestion, not a finding.
8. **"Misery loves company" = Jordan et al.** The paper's title plays on the proverb. The finding is that misery *has* more company than we think, not that unhappy people seek each other out.
