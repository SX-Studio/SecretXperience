Checked 2026-11-27. Every number below was read from the paper itself: a publisher PDF, an author-hosted PDF, a technical report or working paper by the same authors, or the abstract the publisher deposited with Crossref. No pop-science summaries were used as sources. Where I read a pre-publication version and not the typeset article, the section says so.

| 1 | Epley & Whitchurch | Picking an enhanced face is a **tendency, not a rule**. On average people picked only a **6–13% morph** toward the attractive face. ## 1. Epley & Whitchurch (2008): self-recognition enhancement — VERIFIED

**Citation:** Epley, N., & Whitchurch, E. (2008). Mirror, mirror on the wall: Enhancement in self-recognition. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 34*(9), 1159–1170. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167208318601 (PMID 18550861)

**Source read:** Full published PDF from the author's site: https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/699728903166dd3429c6b4fd/699728903166dd3429c6b5ae_EpleyWhitchurch2008.pdf (linked from https://www.nicholasepley.com/publications). I also cross-checked the abstract in Crossref metadata.

**Design:**
- Participants were photographed. Each photo was then morphed in 10% steps (up to 50%) toward a highly attractive composite face or toward an unattractive face, giving 11 images in total: 5 more attractive, 5 less attractive and the real photo.
- 2–4 weeks later participants picked their real photo out of a lineup of all 11 (Exp 1, with a mirror-image follow-up), and did the same for a friend's photo and for strangers' photos (Exp 2).
- In Exp 3 they spotted their own face, as fast as possible, among 11 other faces. Their face appeared as the real photo, a 20% attractive morph or a 20% unattractive morph.

**Key numbers (exact):**
- **Exp 1:** "Caucasian participants (N = 27, 18 female)". The mean morph chosen as "me" was **6.30% toward attractive** (SD 12.45). Test: t(26) = 2.63, p = .02, d = 1.01.
  - Read from Fig. 2 (approximate; not stated in the text): about 37% picked the 10% attractive morph, about 7% the 20% morph and about 11% the 30% morph. About 22% picked the real photo and about 22% picked the 10% *unattractive* morph. That makes roughly 15/27 (about 56%) choosing an attractive version.
  - Likelihood ratings: the 20% attractive morph was rated most likely to be "me" (**M = 65.93%**). The real photo got **M = 54.07%**; paired t(26) = 2.17, p = .04.
  - Self-recognition correlated with implicit self-esteem (r = .47) but not with explicit self-esteem (r = −.26).
- **Exp 1 follow-up (mirror images, N = 15):** mean pick was **12.00%** toward attractive; t(14) = 2.36, p = .03.
- **Exp 2 (Harvard, N = 24):**
  - Self: **13.33%**, t(23) = 4.12, p < .001.
  - Friend: **10.09%**, t(23) = 2.69, p = .01.
  - Strangers (the experimenters): **1.3% and 3.3%**, both ts < 1, not significant.
- **Exp 3 (UChicago, N = 20):** reaction times were:
  - attractive morph: **1,892.09 ms**
  - actual photo: **2,068.44 ms**
  - unattractive morph: **2,133.12 ms**

  The linear trend was significant, F(2, 18) = 4.43, p = .03. Attractive vs actual was "marginally faster", F(1, 19) = 3.35, **p = .08**. Attractive vs unattractive was significant, F(1, 19) = 6.47, p = .02.

**Verbatim (abstract):** "Participants were more likely to recognize an attractively enhanced version of their own face out of a lineup as their own, and they identified an attractively enhanced version of their face more quickly in a lineup of distracter faces. This enhancement bias occurred for both one's own face and a friend's face but not for a relative stranger's face."

**Where the brief is wrong or overstates:**
- "Picked an attractively enhanced version as their real face" is right as a group average and a tendency. It is not what everyone did. About a fifth picked the real photo and about a fifth picked a *less* attractive version.
- The enhancement is small: 6–13% on a scale that runs to 50%.
- "Found it faster in a lineup" is right overall. The difference from the real photo was only marginal (p = .08).
- Pop-science claims such as "20% more attractive" or "65% of people" are wrong. 65.93% is the average *likelihood rating* for the 20% morph.
- Production note: the unattractive targets were photos of people with craniofacial syndromes. Avoid showing or describing them as "ugly faces".

**Safe wording:** "When researchers morphed people's photos to look more or less attractive, people tended to pick a slightly flattering version as their real face — and they did the same for close friends, but not for strangers."

## 2. Quattrone & Tversky (1984): cold-water "heart type" test — PARTIAL (pre-print)

**Citation:** Quattrone, G. A., & Tversky, A. (1984). Causal versus diagnostic contingencies: On self-deception and on the voter's illusion. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 46*(2), 237–248. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.46.2.237

**Source read:** The authors' June 1983 Stanford technical report of the same paper (ONR contract N00014-79-C-0077, DTIC AD-A131586). Its title page says "Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, forthcoming." I read it at https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA131586 and checked the key pages against the scanned page images. **I could not access the typeset 1984 JPSP article** (APA paywall), so the published numbers could differ slightly.

**Design:**
- Stanford undergraduates held their forearm in circulating ice water (about 35°F) for as long as they could. They then pedalled an exercise bike for one minute.
- Next they heard a "psychophysics" mini-lecture about two heart types. Type 1 meant frequent illness and a shorter life. Type 2 meant good health and a longer life.
- Half were told a healthy Type 2 heart *increases* cold tolerance after exercise. The other half were told it *decreases* it.
- They then repeated the cold-water trial with a second experimenter who did not know their condition. Afterwards an anonymous questionnaire asked: "Did you purposefully try to alter the amount of time you kept your hand in the water after exercise?"

**Key numbers (exact, technical report):**
- N = **38** ("21 female and 17 male undergraduates at Stanford University"). All 38 said they would prefer to be Type 2.
- Mean immersion times (Table 1):

  | Condition | Before | After | Change |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Decrease | 44.74 s | 37.11 s | −7.63 s |
  | Increase | 34.21 s | 46.05 s | +11.84 s |

  The interaction was F(1, 36) = 30.64, p < .001.
- "Of the 38 subjects tested, **27 showed the predicted shift**, (13 of 19 in the Decrease condition and 14 of 19 in the Increase condition), and 11 did not". Five per condition did not shift, and one shifted the opposite way.
- "**Only 9 of our 38 subjects** indicated on the anonymous questionnaire that they had purposefully tried to change the amount of time they held their hand in the water after exercise." **29 denied** trying.
- Shifting as predicted was about equally common among admitters (67%) and deniers (72%).
- "Only two of the nine non-deniers (or 22%) inferred having a Type 2 heart, whereas 20 of the 29 deniers (or 69%) inferred a Type 2 heart, p<.05."

**Verbatim (author abstract, in the DTIC/archive.org record):** "As predicted, subjects showed changes in tolerance on the second trial in the direction correlated with a long, healthy life."

**Where the brief is wrong or overstates:**
- The signal of heart type was the **change in tolerance after exercise**, not tolerance itself. Participants were told the two types do not differ before exercise.
- "Most denied" is correct (29/38).
- The "60% of deniers vs 20% of confessors" numbers that circulate online do not match this version (69% vs 22%). Either use the exact counts, or avoid percentages for this sub-finding.
- The sample is small (38 people) and the study is from 1983–84. As far as I can tell, no direct modern replication exists.

**Safe wording:** "In a classic Stanford study, 27 of 38 students changed how long they kept their arm in ice water in whichever direction they'd been told signalled a healthy heart — yet only 9 admitted trying to."

## 3. Karlsson, Loewenstein & Seppi (2009): the ostrich effect — VERIFIED

**Citation:** Karlsson, N., Loewenstein, G., & Seppi, D. (2009). The ostrich effect: Selective attention to information. *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty, 38*(2), 95–115. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11166-009-9060-6

**Source read:** Published Springer PDF (scanned) hosted by CMU: https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/OstrichEffect.pdf. I read the pages as images: pp. 95, 104–107.

**Design:**
- This is a theory paper plus a field test.
- The model predicts that people will check information more when the preliminary news is good, and "put their heads in the sand" when it is bad.
- The test regresses the number of daily account checks on recent market returns in two datasets, controlling for day of week, trend and trading activity.

**Data sources (exact):**
1. **Swedish Premium Pension Authority.**
   - The pension system lets Swedes "choose how to invest 2.5% of their before-tax income". "By 2004, 5.3 million of Sweden's 9 million citizens were in this new pension system."
   - Daily logins ran from **January 7, 2002 to October 13, 2004**, with a mean of **10,903** logins a day, of which 1,142 involved reallocations.
   - "Look-ups" are defined as logins minus reallocations.
   - The benchmark index is the Stockholm All Shares index (OMXSPI).
2. **The Vanguard Group (US).**
   - Daily online account accesses ran from **January 2, 2006 to June 30, 2008**. "In 2007, approximately 21 million investors had accounts at Vanguard."
   - The mean was **416,916** logins a day, with the S&P 500 as the benchmark.

**Key numbers (exact):**
- **Sweden:** "An additional 1% increase (i.e., 0.01) in RETURN_t leads to 120 to 140 additional informational look-ups (i.e., just over 1% of the mean number of daily look-ups)."
- **Vanguard:** "a 1% increase in the prior averaged return (i.e., 0.01) is associated with between 18,000 and 23,000 additional account logins (i.e., 5–6% of the daily mean number of logins)." Also: "Investors log in to their Vanguard accounts much more frequently in rising rather than in falling markets."

**Verbatim (abstract):** "We test for such an 'ostrich effect' in a finance context, examining the account monitoring behavior of Scandinavian and American investors in two datasets. In both datasets, investors monitor their portfolios more frequently in rising markets than when markets are flat or falling."

**Notes:**
- Your description is correct.
- The term "ostrich effect" was coined earlier, by Galai & Sade (2003). This paper is the best-known test of it.
- The data are aggregate daily counts, not tracked individuals.
- A later paper by Sicherman, Loewenstein, Seppi & Utkus used individual-level Vanguard data. That is a different study, so don't merge the two.

**Safe wording:** "Researchers tracking millions of Swedish pension savers and US Vanguard investors found people checked their accounts more after the market rose than when it was flat or falling."

## 4. Chance, Norton, Gino & Ariely (2011): answer-key self-deception — VERIFIED

**Citation:** Chance, Z., Norton, M. I., Gino, F., & Ariely, D. (2011). Temporal view of the costs and benefits of self-deception. *PNAS, 108*(Suppl. 3), 15655–15659. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1010658108 (PMCID PMC3176610, PMID 21383150)

**Source read:** Full text on PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3176610/

**Design:**
- Participants took a short test (math IQ or general knowledge). For some, the answers were printed at the bottom of the page.
- They then predicted their score on a second, similar test that had no answers, and in Exps 2–4 they actually took it.
- Exp 3 paid people for predicting accurately. Exp 4 gave some people a certificate for "above average" scores.

**Key numbers (exact):**
- **Exp 1** (N = 76, MIT; participants scored their own tests):
  - Test 1 score: 6.45 with answers vs 5.58 control (8 items).
  - Predicted correct answers on a hypothetical 100-item test: **81.4 vs 72.7**.
- **Prediction study** (N = 36, Harvard): people *imagining* the answer key did not foresee the inflation. They expected to predict lower without the key (77.9 vs 89.2).
- **Exp 2** (N = 131, UNC; 10-item test):

  | | Answers | Control |
  |---|---|---|
  | Test 1 | 8.97 | 6.29 |
  | Predicted test 2 | 7.73 | 6.32 |
  | Actual test 2 | 5.18 | 4.86 |

  The actual test-2 difference was not significant (t(129) = 1.03, P = 0.31). Higher trait self-deception (BIDR scale) strengthened the inflation.
- **Exp 3** (N = 78, UNC; paid for accuracy, Table 1):

  | | Answers | Control |
  |---|---|---|
  | Test 1 | 7.61 | 4.58 |
  | Predicted test 2 | 7.24 | 4.98 |
  | Actual test 2 | 4.47 | 4.45 |
  | Earnings | **$14.47** | **$17.75** |

- **Exp 4** (N = 136, UNC):
  - Predictions with answers + certificate: **81.2**; answers, no certificate: **64.7**.
  - Control: 52.7 with certificate vs 48.5 without, a non-significant difference.

**Verbatim (abstract):** "We find that those who exploit opportunities to cheat on tests are likely to engage in self-deception, inferring that their elevated performance is a sign of intelligence. This short-term psychological benefit of self-deception, however, can come with longer-term costs: when predicting future performance, participants expect to perform equally well—a lack of awareness that persists even when these inflated expectations prove costly."

**Notes:**
- Your description is accurate.
- Say the answers were "printed at the bottom of the page" rather than "people cheated". The paper itself uses the word "cheating", but participants were not told to avoid the key.
- **Integrity caveat:** Francesca Gino and Dan Ariely have each been the subject of data-integrity findings and retractions on *other* papers. I found no retraction, correction or expression of concern for this paper, and the PMC page shows none. A follow-up by the same team exists: Chance et al., 2015, *Frontiers in Psychology*, "The slow decay and quick revival of self-deception". If the channel wants to be maximally cautious, attribute the study to "Harvard and Duke researchers" and avoid making it the centrepiece.

**Safe wording:** "In one set of experiments, people who had the answers printed on a first test scored higher — then predicted they'd do just as well on a second test without answers, even when overconfidence cost them money."

## 5. Dana, Weber & Kuang (2007): moral wiggle room — VERIFIED

**Citation:** Dana, J., Weber, R. A., & Kuang, J. X. (2007). Exploiting moral wiggle room: Experiments demonstrating an illusory preference for fairness. *Economic Theory, 33*(1), 67–80. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00199-006-0153-z (online 23 Sept 2006)

**Sources read:**
- Published PDF hosted by co-author Kuang's school: https://www.scheller.gatech.edu/directory/research/accounting/kuang/pdf/dwk_2007.pdf
- 2005 working paper (CMU KiltHub): https://kilthub.cmu.edu/articles/journal_contribution/Exploiting_Moral_Wiggle_Room_Experiments_Demonstrating_an_Illusory_Preference_for_Fairness/6571121

The numbers match across both. The p-values below are from the published version.

**Design:**
- University of Pittsburgh students played a binary dictator game. The "dictator" chose between A, which gave **$6 to self and $1 to the other player**, and B, which gave **$5 and $5**.
- In the "hidden information" condition the dictator knew A gave them $6, but not whether it gave the other player $1 or $5. They could reveal the true payoffs with **one free, private click**.
- Other treatments (two dictators; a random cutoff for "plausible deniability") gave similar results.

**Key numbers (exact):**
- **Baseline:** "Of the 19 dictators, 14 (74%) chose B, the ($5, $5) option." 5/19 (26%) chose A.
- **Hidden information (32 dictators):**
  - "only 18 of all 32 dictators (56%) chose to reveal the true payoffs." So **14/32 (44%) chose not to look**.
  - Where the payoffs matched the baseline (16 dictators), "ten (63%) chose A, resulting in the ($6, $1) outcome" [χ²(1) = 4.64, p = 0.03]. In those sessions 8/16 did not look, and all 8 chose A.
  - "only 15 of 32 dictators (47%) revealed the true state and chose the other-regarding option" [p = 0.06].
- **Implemented the fair outcome (Table 5):**

  | Treatment | Fair outcome |
  |---|---|
  | Baseline | 74% |
  | Hidden info (baseline payoffs) | 38% |
  | Multiple dictator | 35% |
  | Plausible deniability | 34% |

  In the authors' words: "roughly 35%, or half of what it is in the baseline."

**Verbatim (abstract):** "We find significantly less generous behavior in these manipulations, relative to a baseline in which the relationship between actions and outcomes is transparent. We conclude that many subjects behave fairly in the baseline case mainly because they intrinsically dislike appearing unfair, either to themselves or others."

**Where the brief is wrong or overstates:**
- "Half chose not to look" is not quite right. It was 44% overall (50% in the sessions with baseline payoffs).
- Stakes were a few dollars, and the samples were small (19 and 32 dictators).
- The paper says roughly a third behave fairly regardless. Don't imply that "everyone" is secretly selfish.

**Safe wording:** "When the fair choice was obvious, 74% of people chose it. When they could stay ignorant of how their choice affected someone else — even though finding out was free — 44% chose not to look, and fair outcomes roughly halved."

## 6. Oster, Shoulson & Dorsey (2013): Huntington disease testing — VERIFIED (published abstract) + pre-print numbers

**Citation:** Oster, E., Shoulson, I., & Dorsey, E. R. (2013). Optimal expectations and limited medical testing: Evidence from Huntington disease. *American Economic Review, 103*(2), 804–830. https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.103.2.804

**Sources read:**
- Published abstract, via Crossref and RePEc.
- The AER-formatted manuscript hosted by the AEA: https://aeastaging.aeaweb.org/conference/2013/retrieve.php?pdfid=52. Its abstract matches the published one word for word.
- NBER Working Paper 17629 (Dec 2011): https://www.nber.org/papers/w17629

I could not access the final typeset AER PDF, which is paywalled.

**Design:**
- The data come from the PHAROS study: **1,001** people at 50% risk for Huntington disease (a parent with HD), all untested at enrollment, at about 40 sites in the US and Canada, followed from 1999 to 2010.
- About every nine months, researchers recorded whether participants had been tested, their self-rated chance of carrying the gene, a clinician's motor-sign rating and major life choices (pregnancy, retirement and so on).
- The clinician's ratings were not shared with participants.

**Key numbers (exact, AER manuscript):**
- "In the ten years that the PHAROS study ran, about 7 percent of individuals with uncertain HD status chose to take an HD test. Testing is even more limited, about 5 percent, when we focus on people who test prior to observing any signs or symptoms of HD."
- In the intro: "fewer than 10 percent of individuals pursue predictive testing during the study." The NBER abstract says: "less than 10 percent of at-risk individuals are tested prior to the onset of symptoms."
- Among untested people whose clinician saw "Certain Signs of HD (>99% confidence)", "the average perceived chance of having HD is 52 percent and 11 percent of individuals in this group report believing there is no chance they carry the HD expansion."
- Retirement was "more than twice as likely for individuals who report knowing they carry the HD expansion versus those who are certain they do not. However, retirement rates for individuals with intermediate probabilities are identical to those who are certain they do not carry the expansion."
- Reasons for not testing: 60% said "a preference for living with uncertainty" was an important reason, against 20% for financial cost and 8% for time cost.
- Testing to *confirm* was more common: 30% of those with acknowledged symptoms had tested (75% in a separate dataset, COHORT).

**Verbatim (published abstract):** "Although genetic testing is perfectly predictive and carries little economic cost, presymptomatic testing is rare. Testing rates increase with increases in ex ante risk of having HD. Untested individuals express optimistic beliefs about their health and make decisions (e.g., retirement) as if they do not have HD, even though individuals with confirmed HD behave differently."

**Notes:**
- Your description is right.
- Quote "about 5–7%" or "fewer than 1 in 10", not a precise single figure.
- The sample is selected (only people who had not yet tested), though the authors say rates resemble the wider HD population.
- The paper argues that avoiding the test can be *rational* for these individuals under an "optimal expectations" model. Narrate it with empathy, not as foolishness.

**Safe wording:** "Among about a thousand people at 50% risk of Huntington's disease, fewer than one in ten took a cheap, definitive genetic test over a decade — and untested people mostly lived as if they didn't carry the gene."

## 7. Ditto & Lopez (1992): motivated skepticism (TAA saliva test) — VERIFIED

**Citation:** Ditto, P. H., & Lopez, D. F. (1992). Motivated skepticism: Use of differential decision criteria for preferred and nonpreferred conclusions. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 63*(4), 568–584. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.63.4.568

**Source read:** Full published PDF (APA scan) on a UNC course page: https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/jpsp-1992-Ditto.pdf. I read pp. 575–578 as page images.

**Design (Study 2, the saliva test):**
- Kent State students learned about a made-up enzyme condition, "TAA deficiency", said to make people "relatively susceptible to a variety of pancreatic disorders" later in life.
- Some were told the test strip **would not change colour** if they were deficient (bad news). Others were told no colour change meant they were fine.
- **The strip was yellow construction paper and never changed for anyone.**
- Participants were secretly videotaped while they took the test. Coders timed how long each person took to decide the test was finished, and noted any retesting.
- Study 3 (N = 79) repeated the idea with a colour change for everyone. What varied was whether the result was described as healthy or unhealthy.

**Key numbers (exact):**
- **Study 2:** 51 recruited; "The final sample consisted of 48 subjects". Video measures cover 47, because one participant was not videotaped.
  - Decision time: "Deficiency subjects took almost 30 s longer on average to decide that their TAA test was complete (**M = 104.8 s**) than did no-deficiency subjects (**M = 76.5 s**), t(45) = 2.60, p < .02." Table 1 prints the second figure as **75.5 s**, an internal typo in the paper, so say "about 30 seconds longer".
  - Retesting: "Thirteen of 25 deficiency subjects (**52%**) engaged in some sort of retesting behavior as opposed to only 4 of 22 no-deficiency subjects (**18%**)". Some re-dipped the strip "up to 12 times".
  - Rated test accuracy (1–9 scale): **4.9** with bad news vs **6.2** with good news.
- **Study 3** (93 recruited, 79 analysed), after the result:
  - Unhealthy-result people listed more life irregularities that could have skewed the test (**M = 1.7 vs .5**).
  - They rated the test as less accurate (**M = 5.6 vs 7.3**).
  - People who answered *before* getting the result did not differ.

**Verbatim (abstract):** "In Studies 2 and 3, Ss given an unfavorable medical test result took longer to decide their test result was complete, were more likely to retest the validity of their result, cited more life irregularities that might have affected test accuracy, and rated test accuracy as lower than did Ss receiving more favorable diagnoses."

**Where the brief is wrong or overstates:**
- The phrase "Waited longer for the test strip to change" needs fixing. Nobody's strip changed. For the bad-news group, *no change* was the bad result, so they took about 30 seconds longer to accept that the test was finished.
- "Retested more" is correct (52% vs 18%).

**Safe wording:** "In a fake saliva test for a made-up enzyme condition, students told that 'no colour change' meant bad news spent about 30 seconds longer deciding the test was done — and over half retested, versus under a fifth of those told it was good news."

## 8. von Hippel & Trivers (2011): background thesis — VERIFIED (abstract)

**Citation:** von Hippel, W., & Trivers, R. (2011). The evolution and psychology of self-deception. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 34*(1), 1–16. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X10001354

**Source read:** The publisher abstract deposited with Crossref (https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1017/S0140525X10001354). I did not read the full target article.

**Verbatim (abstract, first sentence):** "In this article we argue that self-deception evolved to facilitate interpersonal deception by allowing people to avoid the cues to conscious deception that might reveal deceptive intent."

**One-line thesis:** Self-deception may have evolved because believing your own spin makes you a more convincing persuader. It also spares you the mental effort of lying and lowers the punishment if you're caught.

**Safe wording:** "One influential theory — von Hippel and Trivers — argues self-deception may have evolved because the best way to convince others is to believe it yourself." Present this as a theory, not an established fact. The article is a BBS target paper with many published critical commentaries.

## What I could not verify

- **Quattrone & Tversky:** I could not get the typeset 1984 JPSP article. All numbers come from the authors' 1983 technical report of the same paper. They are probably the same but cannot be guaranteed.
- **Oster et al.:** I could not get the final typeset AER PDF. Numbers come from the AEA-hosted AER-format manuscript and the NBER working paper. The published abstract was verified.
- **von Hippel & Trivers:** I verified the abstract only.
- **Epley & Whitchurch, Exp 1 percentages by face:** I estimated these from the bars in Fig. 2. They are not stated in the text. If you use them, say "roughly a fifth" and similar, not exact percentages.
- **Chance et al.:** I found no integrity notice, but searches cannot prove that none exists. The PNAS article page, Retraction Watch and PubPeer are the places to check before publishing.
