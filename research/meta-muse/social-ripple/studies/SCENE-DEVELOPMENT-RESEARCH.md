# Scene Development & Story Engagement for 3–6 Minute Animated Stories

Research for Social Ripple, 2026-11-07. Problem: half of viewers leave by 0:05, and the average view is ~30 s of ~4 min. `VISUAL-ENERGY-RESEARCH.md` covers *pictures per second*. This document covers *reasons to keep watching*: scenes that turn, questions that stay open, and research that arrives as drama.

**Labels**
- **[CRAFT]** established screenwriting or animation practice. It is expert opinion, not experimental evidence.
- **[RESEARCH]** a published study. Its strength is marked *established*, *mixed* or *single study*.
- **[OBS]** our own reference studies.
- **[INF]** my inference or recommendation.

No statistic below is invented. Every number of seconds or beats is marked [INF].

## 1. What a scene must do

- **[CRAFT] A scene turns a value.** McKee defines a scene as "an action through conflict in more or less continuous time and space" that "turns the value-charged condition of a character's life on at least one value". If the value is the same at the end as at the start, the scene is "a nonevent": "Why is this scene in my script?" ([McKee](https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/)). Values are things a person can gain or lose, such as safety, connection or hope.
- **[CRAFT] Scene and sequel.** In Swain's model (via [Ingermanson](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/writing-the-perfect-scene/)), a scene runs **goal → conflict → disaster**. The **sequel** that follows runs **reaction → dilemma → decision**, and the decision sets the next goal ("not a time for action, it's a time for re-action").
- **[CRAFT] Enter late, leave early.** William Goldman: "You always come into the scene at the last possible moment" ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/start-late-leave-early)). A useful refinement: enter with conflict, and leave once the emotion has changed ([The Story Department](https://www.thestorydepartment.com/screenwriting-in-late-out-early/)).
- **[CRAFT] Link scenes with "but" or "therefore", never "and then"** (Parker & Stone, NYU 2011, [TheWrap](https://thewrap.com/south-park-creators-visit-nyu-class-lessons-storytelling-30806)). Andrew Stanton: "If things go static, stories die" ([TED transcript](https://singjupost.com/the-clues-to-a-great-story-by-andrew-stanton-at-ted-full-transcript/)).
- **[INF] At our length a scene is 10–30 s.** Shrink each sequel to one held shot and one line, but keep the *decision*. The decision is where a silent stand-in becomes active.

**Rule [INF]:** every scene card names:
- YOU's goal;
- the obstacle;
- the value at the start and at the end;
- what the ending makes YOU want next.

If the value is the same at both ends, cut or merge the scene.

## 2. Beats inside a scene

- **[CRAFT] A beat is one exchange of action and reaction.** Beats shape the turn. The **turning point** is the beat where expectation and result split apart ([summary of McKee](https://www.shortform.com/blog/five-elements-of-a-story/)).
- **[CRAFT] Yorke's fractal:** acts are built from scenes and scenes from beats, each with the same three-part shape ([Script Blog](https://thescriptblog.com/on-john-yorkes-into-the-woods)). A 20 s scene needs its own setup, turn and landing.
- **[CRAFT] Reversal:** the strongest turn is when an action produces the *opposite* of what was expected.
- **[CRAFT] The button** is the last line or image of a scene. In sitcoms it is a closing joke "built through the entire scene" that carries you into the next ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/what-is-a-button-in-screenwriting); [Sitcom Geek](https://sitcomgeek.blogspot.com/2012/10/finishing-your-scene-on-joke.html)). Ours can be a sting image, a face or a question. Nothing comes after it.
- **[INF] Beat budget.** We narrate at about 150 words/min (≈2.5 words/s) and cut every 2–4 s, so:

  | Scene length | Beats | Shape |
  |---|---|---|
  | 10 s | 2–3 | setup → turn → button |
  | 20 s | 4–5 | goal → push → push-back → turn → button |
  | 30 s | 6–8 | maximum, and it needs an internal reversal |

  A "beat" with no visible reaction (a face, the weather or a prop) is only a narration line.

## 3. Episode structure with a research payload

| Model | Shape | Use for us |
|---|---|---|
| **Save the Cat / three acts** [CRAFT] | Opening image → catalyst → fun and games → midpoint → all is lost → finale → **final image, "opposite of opening image, proving visually that a change has occurred"** ([Reedsy](https://blog.reedsy.com/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet)) | Opening and final images, midpoint, low point |
| **Story circle** (Harmon) [CRAFT] | You → need → go → search → find → take (pay a price) → return → change ([Boords](https://boords.com/blog/storytelling-101-the-dan-harmon-story-circle)) | YOU's arc. Power = go; Ana's storm = find; YOU's own weather = take |
| **Kishōtenketsu** [CRAFT] | Intro → development → **twist** → reconciliation. The drama comes from an unexpected *relationship* between the twist and what came before; conflict is optional ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu)) | The research. A finding works best as a twist that re-reads what the viewer already saw |
| **"What if" premise** [OBS] | Power → rules → test → escalate → cost → choice → callback (our Bearimy, Yuji and Ifverse studies) | The rhythm the genre's viewers expect |
| **Story spine** [CRAFT] | Once upon a time / every day / but one day / because of that / until finally (Kenn Adams; Pixar rule #4) ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5750619); [Pixar rules](https://laughingsquid.com/22-rules-of-storytelling-by-pixar-storyboard-artist/)) | A causality test for the plot |

**Where the fact lands [INF]:**
- as a **turning point**: the character believes X, the world shows not-X, *then* the study is named;
- as a **tool** a character uses to choose;
- as a **twist** that reframes the hero's fear;
- **never as a lecture**, meaning before anything has made us want it.

**Setups and payoffs [CRAFT].** Every payoff needs a plant in the first third that is noticeable but unexplained: an object, a line, or a rule with fine print. Yorke: "the 'truth' of the story lies in the midpoint", and the second half mirrors the first. At the midpoint the hero's assumption flips. At the low point the hero's method stops working.

**Misdirection [OBS]:** in the railroad Short, the viewer believes the wrong theory until the science arrives as the twist (`STUDY-NOTES-2026-11-06.md`).

## 4. Character arcs in miniature

- **[CRAFT] Want vs need.** The want is a conscious goal; the need is an inner change; the flaw blocks the need. Stanton: "all well-drawn characters have a spine", "an itch that they can't scratch". Pixar rule #1: "You admire a character for trying more than for their successes."
- **[INF] A 4-minute arc is one belief changing.** It needs a belief that the ending disproves and one choice that costs something. Here is YOU's arc:

  | Element | YOU in Ep7 |
  |---|---|
  | Belief | "Mine is probably the worst, and saying so would be a burden." |
  | Want | Fix everyone else's weather. |
  | Need | Let one person see yours. |
  | Flaw | The automatic "fine" thumbs-up. |
- **[INF] The silent YOU acts through the body:** a thumb stopping halfway, a deleted text retyped, sitting down instead of walking on. A stand-in who only watches is a camera, not a character.
- **[INF] The supporting character's secret should mirror the hero's flaw.** Ana is the helper who hides, so the advice YOU gives her can be turned back on YOU.
- **[CRAFT + INF] Making Ana matter in under a minute:**
  - a **save-the-cat** act we see before we hear about her;
  - **one vivid detail** that doubles as a plant (boxes labelled "MUM — KITCHEN");
  - **one line in her own words** ("I didn't want to bother anyone");
  - **a want with a deadline** (Saturday).

## 5. Engagement science: established vs speculative

| Mechanism | Evidence | Status | Use [INF] |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Curiosity / information gap** | Loewenstein (1994): curiosity arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's knowledge" and needs some prior knowledge ([record](https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/); [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/)). Answers people were curious about were better remembered 11–16 days later ([Kang et al. 2009](https://neuroecon.berkeley.edu/papers/papers_files/Kang_PsychSci_2009.html)). Curiosity states also boosted memory for incidental material ([Gruber et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494)). | Theory established; memory effect from lab trivia studies | **Ask before telling.** Pose the question a few seconds before the research answer. |
| **Open loops** | A 2025 meta-analysis found **no** memory advantage for unfinished tasks (the Zeigarnik effect), but did find a general urge to *resume* them ([Ghibellini & Meier](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)). | Memory claim weak | Open loops work as *wanting to finish*. Don't cite Zeigarnik. |
| **Suspense vs surprise** | Hitchcock: an unannounced bomb gives "fifteen seconds of surprise"; show it first and you get "fifteen minutes of suspense". His own exception: "when the surprise is a twist". The idea predates him, and he didn't always follow it ([Bordwell](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2013/11/29/hitchcock-lessing-and-the-bomb-under-the-table/)). Brewer & Lichtenstein link suspense to a delayed outcome, curiosity to a withheld cause and surprise to the unexpected ([1982](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/17648/bitstreams/63415/data.pdf)), but later tests found the structures blend rather than produce one emotion each ([UvA](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2559022/169246_05_1_.pdf)). Spoiler studies conflict ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/spoiler-alert-stories-are-not-spoiled-byspoilers.html) vs [Levine 2016](https://news.uark.edu/articles/38085/fulbright-college-research-team-explores-how-spoilers-affect-reading-enjoyment)). | Craft consensus; data mixed | Suspense for stakes (Saturday is coming); surprise for the research twist. |
| **Transportation** | People more absorbed in a story are more affected by it, though some of the authors' own manipulations failed ([Green & Brock 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701)). In a meta-analysis of 132 effects, transportation was strongest with identifiable characters, imaginable plots and realism ([van Laer et al. 2014](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/)). | Established | Use concrete, drawable detail rather than abstraction ("struggling"). |
| **Narrative persuasion** | Narratives shift beliefs (r = .17) and attitudes (r = .19) ([Braddock & Dillard 2016](https://www.aminer.cn/pub/5c7553fcf56def9798637551/meta-analytic-evidence-for-the-persuasive-effect-of-narratives-on-beliefs-attitudes)). | Established; small | Pair the story with one doable action. |
| **Lower resistance** | Entertainment can reduce counter-arguing through identification and transportation ([Moyer-Gusé 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2885.2008.00328.x)). In a 2010 test, transportation went with *more* counter-arguing. | Theory; mixed | A lecture signals intent to persuade, so keep lessons inside choices. |
| **Identification and "you"** | Identification means experiencing a story "from the inside" ([Cohen 2001](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2129552065)). First-person stories about in-group characters increased "experience-taking" ([Kaufman & Libby 2012](https://news.osu.edu/losing-yourself-in-a-fictional-character-can-affect-your-real-life/)). Readers took the actor's view with "you" and "I", but a short preceding context sentence shifted "I" to an outside view ([Brunyé et al. 2009](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/02/090218135124.htm)). | Single studies; applying them to video is **speculative** | Second person helps only if YOU *does* what the viewer would do. |
| **Emotional contagion** | People mimic others' expressions and their feelings follow ([Hatfield et al. 1993](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/1467-8721.ep10770953/)). | Established face-to-face; cartoons **speculative** | Hold on a readable reaction face at every turn. |
| **Stakes without fear** | Threat changes behaviour only alongside high efficacy, meaning a doable action ([Peters, Ruiter & Kok 2013](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/threatening-communication-a-critical-re-analysis-and-a-revised-me)). | Established | Every stake comes with a small, doable action. |

**[CRAFT] Stanton:**
- "Don't give them four, give them two plus two."
- "It's this well-organized absence of information that draws us in."
- "Drama is anticipation mingled with uncertainty" (William Archer).

## 6. Psychology without lecturing

1. **Show it, then name it.** The viewer experiences the effect, then Nia names the study: Ana predicts nobody will come → three yeses → Flynn & Lake. The citation confirms what the viewer already pieced together.
2. **The power is the lens.** The weather must do the finding's work. YOU guessing low and then seeing more clouds *is* Jordan et al. No charts: the viewer review called them "school presentation" (`r_viewer_render_v1.md`).
3. **Let characters disagree.** Theo: "She said she's fine. Leave it." This voices the viewer's objection and makes "ask once more" a choice.
4. **Show consequences.** Rain falls when Ana admits the truth, and her storm softens to *rain*, not sunshine.
5. **Therapeutic integrity [INF, per `THE-LENS.md`]:**
   - Validate first ("Just weather, like everyone's").
   - Clouds stay in the final image (no toxic positivity).
   - The stakes are a heavy Saturday, never a crisis or a diagnosis.
   - The second ask is gentle, and "no" stays an acceptable answer.
   - Show plenty of sun: people judge others' *good* experiences about right (Jordan et al.). A square full of storms would visually claim "most people are struggling", which `RESEARCH.md` forbids.

## 7. Transitions that pull forward [CRAFT, with INF examples]

- **Hard cut on a question.** The next image is the answer, or a deeper question.
- **Match cut.** YOU's thumbs-up at 0:01 matches the thumb that stops at the climax. Ana's box stack matches Kai's van.
- **Sound bridge.** Ana's thunder rumbles a scene *before* we see her storm.
- **Meanwhile.** Give the viewer something YOU lacks (Hitchcock's bomb). Use it at most once.
- **Time skip with a device.** A chalk forecast board reading "SAT ☁".
- **End on an unresolved image.** A thumb hovering over "send"; blank space above YOU's head.

## 10. Scene checklist (apply before animating)

1. **Value turn:** the value at the start ≠ the value at the end. If not, cut or merge.
2. **YOU's goal** is concrete and visible on screen, not only in the narration.
3. **An obstacle pushes back.** No resistance means montage, not drama.
4. **YOU acts physically** at least once (a gesture, a choice or an object).
5. **But/therefore** links this scene to the last one. An "and then" link gets fixed.
6. **Enter late:** the first shot is mid-action or mid-line, never an arrival or exposition.
7. **Button:** the last 1–2 s land, and nothing comes after them.
8. **An open loop leaves the scene,** with no more than ~30 s without a new question.
9. **Research is shown, then named,** using the narration line verbatim from `RESEARCH.md`. No charts.
10. **Specificity:** at least one drawable detail (a label, a name, a day). Replace "some", "many" and "struggling".
11. **Reaction face at the turn,** held long enough to read (VISUAL-ENERGY §3).
12. **Plants logged:** every payoff has a plant, and every plant has a payoff.
13. **Device rules obeyed:** nothing breaks rules 1–3 without a planted exception.
14. **Therapeutic check:**
    - validate before reframing;
    - no diagnosis or prevalence implication (sun stays in the mix);
    - no fear framing and no instant sunshine;
    - every stake comes with a doable action.
15. **Beat budget:** about 2–3 beats per 10 s, at most 8 per scene, and narration of about 2.5 words/s that fits the scene length.
